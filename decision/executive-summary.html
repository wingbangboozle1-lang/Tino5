What the analysis found

Executive summary

Privacy. No name, street address, parcel or account number appears anywhere on this site. Properties are identified only as Property A (Santa Clara County, to be inherited through a trust) and Property B (San Francisco, already owned). All money is rounded to the nearest $10 so published figures cannot be matched back to a public record. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly as published because they are statewide constants and identify nothing.

1. The property tax bill is the decision, not the house value

Under Proposition 13 the assessed value of Property A rises by at most 2% a year and the current bill is about $7,350. The instant the property changes ownership it is reassessed. Even if the Prop 19 parent-child exclusion is claimed in full, the taxable value in 2045 becomes about $4,932,740 and the bill becomes about $65,890. If the exclusion is not claimed, the taxable value is the full market value ($6,531,230) and the bill becomes about $87,250.

That is a 9x increase with the exclusion and a 12x increase without it. No other line item in this analysis is close to that size.

2. California will not tax the inheritance - the federal government might

Revenue and Taxation Code section 13301 says plainly that neither the state nor any local government may impose 'any gift, inheritance, succession, legacy, income, or estate tax' on a transfer at death. There is no California return to file and no California threshold to cross.

The only death tax that can apply is the federal estate tax, and it applies to the estate, not to the heir. The filing threshold is $15,000,000 for a death in 2026 and $15,480,000 for a death in 2027. Amounts above the threshold are taxed at up to 40%.

3. On the numbers supplied, selling and reinvesting wins - but only just past a testable line

Valued like for like over 2026-2076 at a 5% discount rate and a 7% portfolio return, and crediting each option with the market rent of the home it lets you live in, the 2045 transfer produces: occupy $6,258,610, rent out $4,807,930, sell and reinvest $8,226,790.

The ranking is not robust, and that is the useful finding: it flips at a portfolio return of 4.15% against renting and 5.64% against occupying, and at a gross rental yield on Property A of 4.76%. Those are thresholds you can judge; a forecast of what the market will return is not.

4. Renting Property A out is the weakest of the three unless the rent is far higher than assumed

Renting forfeits the Prop 19 exclusion: the property is reassessed to full market value, the bill roughly doubles again versus occupying, and a house worth several million dollars does not throw off enough rent on a 2.6% gross yield to cover tax, maintenance, insurance and management. The net operating income in the first year after a 2045 transfer is about -$9,300.

Renting only ties selling if the gross yield on the property reaches about 4.76%. That is the number to go and test against real comparable rents.

5. There is no deadline to sell, and the step-up in basis is why

Assets acquired from a decedent take a basis equal to fair market value at death. Selling immediately after the transfer therefore realises almost no gain and almost no tax. Selling ten years later realises ten years of gain. The decision can be made after the transfer, with real numbers in hand - which is the strongest argument against committing today.

The exception is the Prop 19 exclusion, which does have hard clocks: the homeowners' exemption must be filed within one year of the transfer and the BOE-19-P claim within three years.

6. Five inputs drive almost the entire spread, and four of them are currently unknown

The market rent for Property A, the market rent for the San Francisco unit that would be freed, the real home insurance premium for each property, the maintenance profile of Property A, and whether the estate will approach the federal exemption. The 1% surcharge over $1,000,000 that lifts the California top rate from 12.3% to 13.3% is not shown on the FTB rate schedules themselves. It is widely reported and is used here as an assumption; confirm it on the FTB site before relying on it.

Read the break-even table before acting on the ranking. The order of the three options is produced by a model, not by a fact. What is solid is the size of the property tax jump, the absence of any California death tax, the step-up in basis, and the filing deadlines. Everything else is a threshold you should test against your own numbers.
