The decision

Move in, rent it out, or sell it, compared after tax, with the break-evens.

Three paths are compared on the same footing. Each starts when the house actually transfers, runs to 2076, and ends with the house sold and the tax paid, so what is compared is after-tax net worth, not cash flows that are not alike.

Path 1: move in

The heir occupies Property A, claims the homeowners' exemption, and keeps the family-home exclusion. The unit the heir vacates at Property B is let at Property B's own reported per-unit rent. No depreciation is taken, because the house is not a rental. On a later sale, the home-sale exclusion shelters up to $250,000 of gain for a single filer.

Path 2: rent it out

The house is let as a single unit. Depreciation is taken straight-line over 27.5 years on the improvement share of the date-of-death value. Rental losses are passive: they are suspended rather than deducted, then released in full when the property is sold.

The tax treatment of this path is the point most often got wrong. A transfer to a child is excluded from change in ownership only where the property is the child's principal residence and the claim is filed. Absent that, the transfer is reassessed at full cash value, so the assessed-value advantage is gone before the first rent cheque arrives.

What renting out actually costs, in tax terms. The first-year property tax is $69,480 against $57,400 for occupancy, a difference of $12,080. That difference is fixed in dollar terms rather than compounding, because the exclusion protects only the value cap, and the capped portion grows at 2% either way. The larger cost of renting is therefore not the tax. It is the operating cost, the capped rent, and the management burden, which together are what put this path last on the comparison above.

Path 3: sell and reinvest

The house is sold at the stepped-up basis, so no income tax is due on the sale itself. Net proceeds are invested at the current risk-free rate, less tax on the interest. No property tax, insurance, utilities, or maintenance are charged, because the house is gone.

What the comparison says

Selling wins on central assumptions, and it wins by a wide margin, because the after-tax risk-free return available today is high in relation to what a house yields net of its own carrying costs. That is a statement about today's interest rates, not about houses.

The rent-out path is the weakest of the three on central assumptions. It gives up the exclusion, takes on operating costs and management, and the rent it can charge is capped: a covered California tenancy is limited to 5% plus inflation, up to 10% a year, and one of the two cities has its own tighter local cap. It only overtakes selling in one corner of the sensitivity grid, where home values compound at 5% a year and the reinvestment rate is at the low end.

The break-even rent of $13,810 a month is well above a realistic single-unit rent for a house of this value, which is why renting loses. But the break-even is a total figure. Two units at half that rent each would change the picture completely, which is why the legal unit count is one of the five questions that matter most.
