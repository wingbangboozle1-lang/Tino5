Carrying costs

What each house costs to hold, line by line, and which lines are not credible.

Two houses, two very different cost structures. The inherited house carries a small tax bill against a large value, and a large maintenance exposure. The already-owned house carries a tax bill that does not match its assessed value. Both carry insurance estimates that look too low.

Property A: the inherited house

The tax bill is low relative to the value of the house. But the part of that bill that the family-home exclusion actually protects is narrower than it first appears: the exclusion preserves the assessed value only up to the value cap of $1,044,586exact, and the rest of the gap is taxed either way. So the difference between occupying and not occupying is real but modest, and it is the same dollar amount in every transfer year.

Flag F01. An effective rate of 2.14% against a published secured rate of 1.18% is not a rounding problem. Either the bill includes assessments the published rate does not capture, which is common and would explain most of it, or the assessed value used here is not the value the bill was computed on. This needs the bill and the assessment notice side by side, and it is the highest-value administrative item in the desk.

The two houses together are assumed to cost $3,000 a year to insure. The statewide average for a single homeowners' policy plus earthquake cover is about $3,780. Two houses in high-value markets, one an older coastal building, at $1,500 each, is not credible. Either the policies are unusually inexpensive, or they are narrower dwelling-fire policies, or earthquake cover is separate and not counted. The gap matters: a $2,000 understatement per house compounds to more than $150,000 over a thirty-year hold.

The electricity line is negative for Property A because the solar array generates more than the house uses. That is a real cash benefit, but it is fragile two ways. The credit for exported power is worth less under net billing than under the old net-metering tariff, and a future occupant who uses more power, or a second unit, would consume the surplus rather than sell it.

Maintenance

Maintenance is modelled at 1.0% of market value a year for the inherited house and 1.2% for the three-unit property, because three units have three kitchens, three bathrooms, and more roof. This is a planning convention, not a measured figure, and it is the largest operating line for the inherited house. A condominium would carry a lower number plus a monthly association assessment; a detached house on its own lot carries a higher one. Which this is remains an open question.
