Suggestions

Options compared on robustness, with a recommended sequence and three things not to do.

This section is deliberately separate from the analysis. It is the place to compare paths rather than numbers, and to choose one before anything is implemented.

Read this before the table. The analysis shows selling winning on central assumptions. That is a statement about arithmetic, not a statement about what the heir should do, because the arithmetic cannot see housing plans, risk tolerance, or the non-financial value of keeping a family house. Those are exactly what this section is for.

B. Move in and hold long term Occupy the house, keep the exclusion, and treat it as a home and a store of value. Keeps the exclusion and the Proposition 13 hedge, and provides housing with no rent to pay. Concentrates the balance sheet in one illiquid asset in one region, and gives up the compounding the sell path captures. High if the heir genuinely wants to live there
C. Sell and reinvest broadly Sell at the stepped-up basis with no income tax on the sale, and invest the proceeds. Highest net worth in twenty-four of the twenty-seven sensitivity combinations tested, and it removes concentration and carrying-cost risk.Exposed to selling at a cyclical low, and the exclusion is gone permanently.Good on average, worst at the wrong moment
D. Rent it out Let the house and keep it as an income asset. Keeps the asset and produces income. Gives up the exclusion, takes on operating risk, and the rent is capped. It beats selling only when home values compound at 5% a year and the reinvestment rate is low at the same time. Wins in one corner of the grid, loses elsewhere
E. Keep it, and set a decision date Keep the house and commit to deciding by a stated year rather than deciding now. Avoids a forced sale, keeps both options open, and lets the market and the basis mature. Requires discipline, and the carrying cost is real in the meantime. High, and compatible with A

What I would do, in order

  1. Do the deadline work immediately, whichever option is chosen. Claim the homeowners' exemption within the year and the exclusion within three years. This costs a form and preserves the most valuable asset in the analysis. It is not optional and it is not contingent on the decision.
  2. Close the two free gaps in the first month. The deed and the trust document; the two tax bills beside the two assessment notices. Everything downstream depends on them, and neither costs more than an afternoon.
  3. Do not rent the house out before the exemption is claimed. This is the sequencing error that matters, because eligibility is tested, and a rental is evidence against principal-residence use.
  4. Choose between B, C, and E on non-financial grounds, then let the numbers break the tie. The financial gap is large in expectation but sits inside a range that one bad sale date can erase. The difference in lifestyle and concentration is not modelled and is at least as large.
  5. If the answer is C, sell into strength rather than on a deadline. The worst outcome in the stress cases is a forced sale in a bust. A sale with a two-year window and no financing contingency is a materially better sale than one with a date.
  6. If the answer is B or E, document the basis now. A date-of-death appraisal, the improvement receipts, and the permit history. This is the cheapest insurance in the plan and it is worthless if assembled ten years late.

The three things not to do

Do notBecause
Let the exemption claim slip past one year The exclusion is then removed, a new base-year value is enrolled, and the tax bill roughly doubles from the following lien date.
Let the trust question stay unanswered If the house is not in the trust, the estate pays statutory probate fees before any tax.
Rely on a single fifty-year growth number The stress cases move the answer by millions of dollars on assumptions that are all historically defensible.
