Trust process

From a death to a recorded title, in order, with the deadlines that carry real penalties.

The order matters, and two steps have hard deadlines. This is the sequence from a death to a recorded title, on the assumption that the house passes through a trust rather than through probate. If the trust is defective, or the house was never actually transferred into it, the probate route applies, and it is slower and considerably more expensive.

What probate would cost if the trust fails

California sets statutory fees for ordinary probate services on a sliding scale: 4% of the first $100,000 of the estate, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, 1% of the next $9,000,000, 0.5% of the next $15,000,000, and a court-determined amount above $25,000,000. The executor is entitled to the same amount as the attorney. On these numbers the trust is worth more than the tax planning.

The three ways this process goes wrong

  1. The house was never put in the trust. A trust only controls what is titled in its name. A house still in the deceased's individual name passes under the will, which means probate, which means the fees above. This is checked in step one and it is the whole ballgame.
  2. The exemption is claimed late. The one-year homeowners' exemption claim is the difference between keeping the deceased's assessed value and paying tax on today's market value, every year, for as long as the house is held. The statute is explicit: the exclusion is removed on the date the transferee stops being eligible, and a new base-year value is enrolled from the following lien date.
  3. The basis is not documented. A date-of-death appraisal costs a few thousand dollars. Without it, the tax authority can take the position that basis is the original purchase price, and the resulting gain can be enormous.
