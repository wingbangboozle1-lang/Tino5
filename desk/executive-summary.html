Executive summary

The answer, the four levers that control it, and the deadlines that already exist.

The situation in six sentences

  1. A house (called Property A) sits in a family trust and will pass to the heir sometime between 2040 and 2050. Its assessed value is a small fraction of its market value.
  2. That gap is a real, transferable asset, and it survives only if the heir occupies the house as a principal residence and claims the homeowners' exemption within one year of the transfer.
  3. The heir already owns a second house (Property B) outright. It is not part of the inheritance, and its property-tax charge appears to be roughly double what the city's published rate implies.
  4. There is no California inheritance or estate tax. The state's bite comes from reassessment, not from a tax on inheriting.
  5. On central assumptions, selling and reinvesting produces the highest net worth in 2076 in every transfer year tested, because the risk-free rate available today is high in relation to what a house yields net of its own carrying costs.
  6. That ranking is fragile. Holding only catches up if home values compound at about 5.8% a year for the whole horizon, and the sell path is exposed to the risk that the sale is forced at the bottom of a cycle.

The seven claims this desk is most confident about

  1. There is no California estate or inheritance tax. The state's estate tax was repealed effective 1 January 2005.
  2. The federal estate-tax filing threshold is $15,000,000 for a death in 2026.
  3. Inherited property takes a basis equal to its fair market value at the date of death, so a sale at that value realises no gain.
  4. The family-home exclusion adds up to $1,044,586exact to the old base-year value before any reassessment applies.
  5. The exclusion is conditional. It is removed if the transferee stops being eligible for the homeowners' or disabled veterans' exemption.
  6. Rental income is passive income. Rental losses cannot be deducted against salary, and are suspended until the property is sold in full.
  7. The transfer tax on a sale differs by county, and one of the two counties bands its rate by price, so the rate is not a single number.

The three things most likely to be wrong

  1. The insurance figures. Both houses carry owner estimates far below the statewide average premium, and no declaration page has been seen.
  2. Property B's tax charge. The owner's own bill implies a 2.14% effective rate against a published 1.18% secured rate. One of the two numbers is wrong.
  3. Long-run growth. A single 3.5% figure for fifty years is a convenience, not a belief. The stress cases move the answer by millions.
