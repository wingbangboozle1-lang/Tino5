Black swans

Seven sourced stress paths, and what they agree on even when they disagree.

Growth and inflation are never consistent over fifty years. The central case uses one number for each, which is a convenience and not a belief. This page replaces it with seven stress paths, each anchored to an episode or a rule that is documented, and asks the same question of each: does holding the house still beat selling it?

How to read this page. These are not forecasts and they are not equally likely. They are stress paths, chosen because each has a documented anchor. The point is not to predict which one happens. The point is to see which decisions survive all of them, and which do not.
One convention, stated so it can be argued with. The sell path assumes the seller still needs somewhere to live, so it charges rent equal to what the house itself could earn. Without that charge the comparison would credit the seller with the investment return and free housing, which is not a comparison at all.

What the stress paths agree on

FindingWhy it holds across the scenarios
The sell path has a timing risk the hold path does not A sale happens on one day. If that day falls in a bust, the loss is locked in. A hold can wait. The 2006-2011 case is the clearest demonstration: an index decline of about 21% from peak to trough is survivable if you do not have to sell.
Holding wins only in the inflation case That is the whole finding. In the stagflation path the 2% assessment cap turns the property tax into a shrinking real cost while everything else inflates, and that is enough to beat the sell path. In every other path, including the central case, selling wins.
Proposition 13 is the only structural edge holding has No financial asset reproduces a cost that cannot rise faster than 2% a year. Everything else about a house, from maintenance to insurance, rises with or above inflation.
Insurance is the most under-budgeted risk Both houses are assumed at premiums below the statewide average. In the insurance contraction path the annual holding cost in year thirty reaches several hundred thousand dollars.
Occupying and then moving out is not catastrophic Under the statute, losing eligibility enrolls the base-year value established at the original transfer, adjusted annually. So a household that occupies for eight years and then moves out ends up better off than one that never occupied, and both converge on the same tax by year thirty. The mistake is expensive, but it is not a cliff.

The uneven economy, stated plainly

Two facts from the record should shape how much weight any single projection deserves.

  • Inflation has been violent. The consumer price index rose 13.3% in the twelve months to December 1979 and 13.5% over calendar 1980, then 8.0% over calendar 2022. A 2% assumption for fifty years is a central tendency, not a description of any particular decade.
  • House prices have had long flat and falling stretches. The national index fell about 21% from its 2007 peak to February 2011, and took years to recover in real terms. A thirty-five year average of 4.28% conceals that entirely.

The practical consequence is that a plan which requires a sale on a particular date, or which requires home values to compound at a particular rate, is fragile. A plan that keeps options open, and that treats the assessed value as an asset to be protected rather than as a detail, survives all seven paths. That is the case for option A on Suggestions.
