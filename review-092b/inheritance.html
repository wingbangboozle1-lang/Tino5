Step-by-step process

How inheriting through a trust actually works

The sequence is the protection. Three of the steps below have hard deadlines measured from the date of death, not from the date the estate is distributed, and two of them are what stand between you and a property-tax bill based on full market value. Print this page.

The arithmetic of missing step 5

The difference between filing and not filing the homeowners' exemption claim, and between occupying and not occupying, is the whole Prop 19 benefit. At a 2045 transfer the gap is $23,840 a year. Compounded at 2% for 25 years that is about $763,590 of extra tax paid, on a property that would otherwise have been the cheapest of the three paths to hold.

What the trust changes — and what it does not

  • It changes who decides. Under a continuing trust the trustee, not the beneficiary, holds the power to sell. Ask for the distribution article in writing (Q-03).
  • It changes the paperwork. A transfer at death inside a trust is still a transfer for property-tax purposes; the assessor's exclusion claim still has to be filed.
  • It does not change the income-tax basis rule. Property acquired from a decedent takes a basis equal to fair market value at the date of death, whether or not a trust is involved.
  • It does not shelter the estate from the federal test. Assets in a revocable trust are still in the gross estate.

Documents to collect, in one list

  1. Trust instrument and any amendments, plus the schedule of beneficiaries.
  2. Certification of trust accepted by the county recorder.
  3. The most recent property-tax bill for Property A and the assessor's notification of assessed value.
  4. Date-of-death appraisal (or the Form 706 value) for Property A.
  5. Any depreciation schedules if A was ever rented.
  6. The estate inventory, including the cash bequest (Q-04).
  7. Insurance declarations for both properties, and three current quotes for each (Q-05).
  8. The Rent Board Housing Inventory record for Property B (Q-09).
