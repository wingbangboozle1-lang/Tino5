The single most important suggestion

Keep Property B as your home and treat the decision as being about Property A alone. That is not a financial trick; it follows from the structure of the two assets. B is already yours, already protected by its own Proposition 13 base and already producing rent from two units while leaving a third for you. A arrives with a broken tax base, no tenants, and a single-family cost structure. The temptation is to move into the larger, more valuable house. The arithmetic says the value in A is best realised by selling it, and the housing need is already met by B.

If the family preference is to live in A, that is a legitimate reason to choose it — but it should be chosen as a preference, with the cost stated, not as a financial conclusion.

Proposals in detail

S-01 · Keep Property B as the home you live in, in every path

effort Low · cost $0 · impact High · confidence High on facts, medium on money

What to do. Treat B as the housing solution and decide only what happens to A. B already has two leased units and a third you can occupy without losing that rent; A has no such offset. This is the single structural fact that separates the properties.

Why. A is a single-family house that would be reassessed at transfer; B is protected by its Prop 13 base and its rent. Moving into A therefore costs the Prop 19 tax break against the rent from B's third unit and against A's own carrying costs.

What could go wrong. Only if A is where you actually want to live, or if B's third unit is not legally habitable by you (Q-09).

Evidence: Proposition 19

S-02 · Do not sign anything about A before the trust and basis documents are read

effort Low · cost $0 · impact High · confidence High

What to do. Before the transfer, obtain four documents: the trust instrument, the schedule of beneficiaries, the transferor's most recent property-tax bill, and any depreciation schedules if A was ever rented.

Why. Three of the five open Critical questions cannot be answered without them, and the Prop 19 occupancy deadline starts at death, not at distribution.

What could go wrong. None. This is sequencing, not an action on the property.

Evidence: Revenue & Taxation Code §63.2

S-03 · Book the two Prop 19 deadlines on the day of death

effort Low · cost $0 · impact High · confidence High

What to do. The transferee must occupy within one year and file for the homeowners' exemption within one year; the exclusion claim itself can be filed within three years.

Why. If the occupancy and exemption steps are missed, the exclusion is removed and the factored value for the full market value is enrolled from the following lien date. The difference measured here is {gap} a year at a 2045 transfer.

What could go wrong. None; this is a calendar item.

Evidence: Revenue & Taxation Code §63.2

S-04 · Get three insurance quotes per property before modelling any further

effort Medium · cost $0 · impact High · confidence High

What to do. Admitted-carrier quotes plus one California FAIR Plan quote per property, each with the dwelling limit and deductible stated.

Why. Insurance is the only cost still blank on this site. It is also the cost most likely to move sharply: the department's own consumer guides exist because the market is disrupted, and a FAIR Plan policy covers fire only.

What could go wrong. None, but quotes expire; refresh within 90 days of the transfer.

Evidence: Information Guides

S-05 · Publish a private figure sheet, not a public one

effort Low · cost $0 · impact High · confidence High

What to do. Keep the real assessed values, bills and rent roll in a file you hold offline; publish only rounded anchors on this site.

Why. Rounding a single figure protects it; rounding a history does not, because the county roll is open data and a 13-row series is a unique match.

What could go wrong. You lose the ability to share the detail by link. That is the point.

S-06 · Test the sell decision against a lower reinvestment return, not a higher one

effort Low · cost $0 · impact High · confidence Medium

What to do. Run the decision page with the reinvestment return at 3% after tax rather than 5%.

Why. Selling only wins because the proceeds compound. If the after-tax return is 3%, the advantage narrows sharply; at 7% it widens. The assumption is doing more work than any official figure.

What could go wrong. None; this is a robustness check.

Evidence: TSP rates of return

S-07 · Decide the sale before the transfer, but execute it after

effort Medium · cost $0 · impact High · confidence Medium

What to do. Agree with the trustee in advance that A will be sold promptly if the family chooses that path, so the sale is not delayed by probate administration.

Why. The §1014 step-up is what makes an immediate sale tax-free; delay adds market risk without adding tax benefit, and the county reassesses as of the date of death anyway.

What could go wrong. A forced sale in a bad market. Mitigate with a 12–24 month treasury ladder rather than a single-day reinvestment.

Evidence: Publication 551

S-08 · Keep Property B out of the comparison — but do not forget it

effort Low · cost $0 · impact Medium · confidence High

What to do. Report B's own net income and tax separately, exactly as the projections page does, instead of folding it into the A decision.

Why. B is owned in all three paths, so it cancels out of the comparison but not out of your life. Folding it in is how two earlier drafts of this analysis produced contradictory answers.

What could go wrong. None.

Evidence: Learn about rent increases in San Francisco

S-09 · Model the black-swan cases as separate events, not one 'bad case'

effort Medium · cost $0 · impact Medium · confidence High

What to do. Use the five regimes on the black-swan page: stagflation, housing bust, rate shock, demand collapse, and the live disinflation that has not arrived.

Why. They act on different variables. A housing bust hurts the sell path's timing; stagflation hurts the reinvestment path's real return. One blended 'downside' number hides both.

What could go wrong. None, but do not average the regimes into a probability — we have no basis for assigning probabilities.

Evidence: USSTHPI

S-10 · Re-verify four official thresholds every February

effort Low · cost $0 · impact Medium · confidence High

What to do. Prop 19 allowance (BOE letter), CPI inflation factor (BOE letter), federal exclusion (IRS revenue procedure, usually October/November), and the city's allowable rent increase (Rent Board, usually January).

Why. All four change on a published schedule, and three of them are the largest numbers in the model.

What could go wrong. None.

Evidence: LTA 2025/009

S-11 · Do not treat the model's winner as a recommendation to act now

effort Low · cost $0 · impact Medium · confidence High

What to do. Use the decision page as a filter that tells you which questions matter, not as an instruction.

Why. Nine of the model's inputs are assumptions. A model whose largest lever is an assumption cannot be the basis for an irreversible act a decade from now.

What could go wrong. None.

Evidence: Estate tax

What we suggest you do not do

  • Do not publish the assessment histories, even rounded to $10 (Q-01).
  • Do not sell Property B to fund anything. It has no basis step-up and would carry the whole embedded gain, while forfeiting the rent and the housing.
  • Do not rely on a single-point projection. Every figure on this site is a range in disguise; the point estimate is only the midpoint.
  • Do not treat the current law as the 2045 law. Four of the numbers that drive the answer are re-set on a published schedule.

Privacy. No names, street addresses, parcel or account numbers, ZIP codes, phone numbers or e-mail addresses appear anywhere in this site. Both properties are referred to as Property A (to be inherited through a trust) and Property B (already owned). Assessor histories are not published: a series rounded to $10 is still a fingerprint against open county roll data.
