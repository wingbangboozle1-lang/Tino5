Stress conditions that mimic the real record

Black swans and uneven economies

Why this page exists. A 50-year projection that assumes a constant 4% growth rate and a constant 2.5% inflation rate is not a projection, it is a straight line. Growth and inflation have never been constant over any 50-year period. Every number on this page is computed by this site from two official published series — the CPI-U from the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the FHFA all-transactions house-price index — not from a story about what a bad decade might look like.

Five real episodes, measured

Both legs of each episode are shown with their own dates, because the two series need not cover the same years: S1's inflation ran for a decade while nominal house prices only stalled for three years inside it. "Real house price" is the house-price change deflated by the CPI change.

What each episode actually did to each of the three choices

S1 · Stagflation (1973–1982) Prices more than doubled — 8.9% a year — while the national house-price index rose 8.7% in total over its 2.75-year window, a loss of about half the real value. For this decision the lesson is about the reinvestment leg, not the house: a portfolio that does not beat inflation does not preserve the proceeds of a sale.
S2 · Housing bust (2006–2012) The national index fell 15.9% nominally, 2.9% a year, and 26.5% in real terms over six years. A sale forced in 2009 would have realised a quarter less in real purchasing power than a sale in 2006. This is the argument for the treasury ladder: sell when you decide to sell, then reinvest over 12–24 months rather than on one day.
S3 · Rate shock (2022–2023) The policy rate rose 400 basis points in nine months, and the national house-price index still rose 1.0%. The shock hit affordability and transaction volume, not prices. Do not assume a rate rise forces a price fall.
S4 · Demand collapse (2020) The twelve-month CPI change in April 2020 was effectively zero — 0.1% on CPI-U, −0.1% on CPI-W — and the housing index then gained 58.7% in six years. A cash-flow shock and an asset-price shock are different events. If you rent A out, the cash-flow shock is the one that matters, and the rent cap in the city where B sits limits your ability to recover it.
S5 · The live one (2026) CBO's February 2026 baseline had PCE inflation at 2.7% for 2026 and 2.0% from 2030. CPI-U rose 3.7% in the year to September 2026 and 3.4% to October, and the FOMC raised its target range 25 basis points on 17 September 2026 after three cuts in 2025. Over the whole 2021–2026 stretch inflation has run 4.3% a year, so the 2.5% inflation assumption used in the central case is optimistic relative to the last five years.

Re-running the decision under each regime

Each regime's own annual rates are substituted for the model's assumptions for as many years as the episode lasted, and the model then reverts to its central assumption. Applying a two-year price fall across twenty-five years would be an invented forecast, not a stress test. During each episode the reinvestment leg is assumed to earn only the regime's own inflation — no real return at all — which is deliberately unkind to the sell path; after the episode it reverts to the central 5%. This tests the ordering of the three choices; it does not assign probabilities.

What the stress shows. Selling survives four of the five episodes even with a zero real return on the proceeds, because after the episode the model reverts to central assumptions. It loses only where housing ran far ahead of everything else — the 2020s boom, S4 and S5 — and there the winner is renting A out, not occupying it. The column that matters is the last one: each episode moves the break-even reinvestment return to somewhere between 2.8% and well above 5%, and that range is wider than any of the differences between the three paths.

The last two rows place the model's assumptions beside the official history they are meant to represent. Housing at 4.0% nominal is very close to the national 4.3% since 1980; but the national index is not this house, and the two decades since 2000 are the only stretch in which the index behaved that way.

The projection in this site is not a straight line either

Two of the model's own mechanisms already break the straight line: property tax is capped at a 2% factor while market values grow at 4%, so the effective tax rate on value falls every year; and cost escalation is driven by the inflation assumption independently of growth. What the model does not contain is a single year in which any series falls. That is the gap this page exists to make visible, and it is why the stress test above is not a probability-weighted average.
