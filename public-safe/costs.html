Insurance: what the official data does and does not say

The California Department of Insurance publishes a market snapshot, not quotes. It reports an average California homeowner premium of $1,571 against a national average of $1,512, with 677,449 FAIR Plan homeowner policies and 662 ZIP codes in distressed areas (CDI · Sustainable Insurance Strategy). None of those numbers is a premium for either property.

  • A $1,500 homeowner premium is below the state average and is not plausible for a dwelling whose replacement cost is well over seven figures. The model uses $3,000 and tests $9,000 on the stress page.
  • A three-unit building needs a landlord policy, not a homeowner policy. Coverage, liability and loss-of-rent terms differ, and the premium is not comparable to A's.
  • FAIR Plan is a last resort and covers only fire, lightning, internal explosion and smoke; extended coverage and a separate differences-in-conditions policy are usually needed (CDI · Residential insurance).
  • Earthquake is separate. Nothing in this analysis assumes earthquake cover.

Water, power and solar

  • Water. The owner's ranges imply about $1,500 a year for A and $4,380 for B. Provider tariffs, sewer charges, meter size and irrigation are unverified.
  • Electricity. A's solar is reported to produce a small annual credit. Exports are credited at the applicable tariff, which for systems interconnected after April 2023 is the net billing tariff with export credits usually below retail (CPUC · Net energy metering & net billing). Whether an existing tariff survives a change of ownership is not established here — that is the single largest risk to the "no electricity cost" assumption.
  • Inverter and battery replacement are in the capital reserve, not in the electricity line.

Maintenance rule of thumb used

Routine maintenance is modelled at $9,000 for A and $7,000 for B, plus capital reserves of $3,000 and $3,500. That is far below the common "1% of value" rule for A ($31,000), because that rule scales with land value in a high-price market and overstates upkeep on a maintained house. It is an assumption, and the low-growth stress case shows what happens if it is wrong.
