Decision framework

Occupy, rent, or sell — the comparison that decides it

Property B is retained in every path. In the rent and sell cases the owner can remain in B. In the occupy-A case the owner moves out of B, freeing its third unit. No rent is counted for that unit because legal status, condition and market rent are unverified. Thus the A-only occupancy figure is not a housing-equivalent wealth comparison.

The result

Selling A is ahead of renting by $5,566,300 and ahead of A-only occupancy cash flows (excluding possible extra B rent) by $10,870,900 by 2070. Renting only wins if the reinvested portfolio earns less than 3.01% after tax, or if rent grows faster than 5.12% a year for 25 years.

Why selling wins here — and why that is not a general rule

  • Basis steps up, so the exit is cheap. Inherited property takes a date-of-death basis (IRS · Inherited property basis). Selling near that value produces almost no gain, so the only friction is selling cost.
  • Prop 19 punishes a rental heir. The parent-child exclusion requires the home to be the transferee's principal residence. Rent it out and the taxable value becomes the full market value: $6,531,230 at 2045, tax $81,640 (BOE · Proposition 19).
  • Bay Area rental yields are thin at these prices. Gross rent is 1.77% of value at 2045 before costs — the reason the net result is -$7,040.
  • Occupying converts an asset into consumption. The A-only gap is $10,870,900 over 25 years, before any additional rent from the vacated unit at B. Without a verified third-unit rent, it is not a price of occupancy or a personalized optimum.
  • Property B is different. It is already owned, so there is no step-up and no reassessment. Its tax basis is unknown: proceeds before income tax would be $1,687,500; capital-gains tax and after-tax investable proceeds are blank pending basis records. Compare against $53,430 in existing net rent before income tax (IRS · Publication 551 · SF Treasurer · Secured property tax rate).

One offset not in the table

If you moved into A, the third unit at B could in principle be let, adding rent that the table does not count. At a placeholder $36,000 a year growing 3.0%, that would narrow the gap to selling by roughly $1,312,530. This simple gross-rent illustration ignores expenses, income tax and reinvestment of receipts; the placeholder is not a rent estimate, and letting the unit depends on its legal status and condition — both unverified (CA Civil Code 1947.12).
