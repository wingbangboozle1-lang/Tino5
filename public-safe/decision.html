Decision framework

Occupy, rent, or sell — the comparison that decides it

Property B already provides housing at no rent, so all three paths assume the same housing outcome. That removes lifestyle from the comparison and leaves tax, cash flow and returns.

The result

Selling A is ahead of renting by $5,886,890 and ahead of occupying by $9,166,400 by 2070. Renting only wins if the reinvested portfolio earns less than 2.91% after tax, or if rent grows faster than 5.69% a year for 25 years.

Why selling wins here — and why that is not a general rule

  • Basis steps up, so the exit is cheap. Inherited property takes a date-of-death basis (IRS · Inherited property basis). Selling near that value produces almost no gain, so the only friction is selling cost.
  • Prop 19 punishes a rental heir. The parent-child exclusion requires the home to be the transferee's principal residence. Rent it out and the taxable value becomes the full market value: $6,531,230 at 2045, tax $81,550 (BOE · Proposition 19).
  • Bay Area rental yields are thin at these prices. Gross rent is 1.77% of value at 2045 before costs — the reason the net result is -$6,960.
  • Occupying converts an asset into consumption. It is rational if living there is worth about $9,166,400 over 25 years — roughly $366,660 a year — but it is a purchase, not an investment.
  • Property B is different. It is already owned, so there is no step-up and no reassessment. Its low basis makes a sale expensive: proceeds $1,687,500, illustrative gain tax $549,640, net $1,137,860 — which would then have to beat $53,430 of existing net rent plus appreciation to be worth doing (SF Treasurer · Secured property tax rate).

One offset not in the table

If you moved into A, the third unit at B could in principle be let, adding rent that the table does not count. At a placeholder $36,000 a year growing 3.0%, that would narrow the gap to selling by roughly $1,387,910. It would not close it. The placeholder is not a rent estimate, and letting the unit depends on its legal status and condition — both unverified (CA Civil Code 1947.12).
