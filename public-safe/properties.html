What is known about each property — and what is not

Two properties, opposite starting points. A is a future inheritance with a low taxable value and a high market value. B is already owned, with a very low taxable value and rent-controlled tenants; its income-tax basis is probably low but is not verified. Confusing the two produces bad advice, so they are kept separate throughout.

The distinction that drives everything

A is inherited. Its income-tax basis steps up to date-of-death value, and its property-tax assessment is reset unless the heir occupies it and claims the Prop 19 family-home exclusion. B is already owned. Inheriting A does not change B's basis, its assessment, its tenants or its rents. Any plan that sells B "because of the inheritance" is solving a problem that does not exist.

Holding B keeps $53,430 of net rent, keeps the housing, and preserves a future step-up for the next generation; selling converts it into a taxable portfolio and a large, one-off tax bill.

What is still unknown

  • Trust terms, successor-trustee powers, and whether A is included in the transferor's gross estate.
  • Whether either property carries a mortgage, reverse mortgage, lien or deferred maintenance.
  • Actual B leases, security deposits, rent-control registration and the legal status of the third unit.
  • Whether the solar system's net-metering tariff survives a change of ownership (CPUC · Net energy metering & net billing).
  • Actual insurance quotes, water and power tariffs, and property-tax direct assessments.
