Selling into a crash: does parking the money first help?

The owner asked for a phased-investing path in the black-swan work: park the sale proceeds in 3-month to 2-year Treasuries for 24–36 months after the inheritance, then phase into stocks, and test whether that softens the risk of entering the market at the top of a crash. This page does exactly that and reports the result against the alternative on the same footing.

The rule being tested

Sell the house. Hold the proceeds in Treasury bills and notes with maturities of three months to two years for 24 or 36 months. Then move the money into equities in equal monthly tranches over the following twelve months. Compare against selling and investing the whole amount on the first day.

What the table says

Parking is insurance with a price. In the two crash states it adds $874,530 to $1,334,820 per $1,000,000 sold under the 1929 shape and $1,486,590 to $1,264,250 under the 2008 shape. In the baseline it costs $200,810 per $1,000,000, and in the strong-growth state it costs $1,551,270. Twenty-four months beats thirty-six in three of the five states, because the extra year out of the market is pure cost when nothing falls.

Reinvestment risk, stated plainly

The risk the phasing does not remove. Parking the proceeds moves the reinvestment risk, it does not delete it. At the end of the parking window the heir still has to invest, and the market can fall then instead. A 1929-shaped path has three successive down years, so a 24-month park lands the heir in the third one and still helps; a V-shaped fall that bottoms during the parking window and recovers before it ends leaves the heir buying higher than if they had never parked. The honest description is a trade: certain small cost in ordinary times against a large gain in one particular shape of crash.

The policy this implies, if the household wants the insurance

  • Write it before the money arrives. The decision is easy in hindsight and hard in the moment; a written rule is the only defence against selling at the bottom.
  • Choose the window by the measurement, not by fear. 24 months dominated 36 in three of the five states tested here.
  • Park only what will be invested. Money earmarked for tax, fees or a reserve should not be in the phased sleeve at all.
  • Set the tranche schedule and the end date. Equal monthly tranches over twelve months is the rule modelled here; any other rule should be measured the same way.
  • Name the person who executes it. A policy without an executor is a wish.

Scope. A decision aid for one household question: when the inherited house arrives, should the heir live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest? Every figure is a model output from a round modelling base — not either property's value — rounded to $10. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly as published and marked. Nothing here is legal, tax or investment advice.

Privacy. No names, streets, parcel numbers, postcodes, cities or neighbourhoods appear on this site, and no figure from the household's own records is published at any precision. The conclusions are published as break-evens, rankings and rates, which move only slightly across a tenfold range of property values — the small movement is caused by a fixed statutory cap and is tested and stated on the method page.
