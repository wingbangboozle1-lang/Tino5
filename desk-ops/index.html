What would change the answer

One house is expected through a trust between 2040 and 2050. A second, already owned, is a three-unit building with two units let. The question is what the heir does with the inherited house: live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest. This page answers in the only order that survives scrutiny — first which measurements decide it, then what the answer is.

Five things decide this, and nothing else does

  1. House-price growth. Holding the house and selling it tie at 10.49% a year in the base case. Below that, selling wins; above it, holding wins. That single number is the whole argument. It is a break-even, not a forecast.
  2. Whether the heir really moves in — and stays. The intergenerational exclusion is worth $-14,620 a year at the top of the local rate range on the modelling base. Losing it after the fact costs $10,067,890 over twenty years. RTC 63.2
  3. What the money would otherwise earn. At the base case the other option — selling and investing — beats living in the house unless equities return less than 4.83% a year, or rent is worth more than 5.0% of the house's value to the heir.
  4. When the equity market is entered. If the sale is followed by a 1929-shaped or 2008-shaped crash, parking the proceeds in 3-month to 2-year Treasuries for 24–36 months before phasing in adds $874,530 to $1,334,820 per $1,000,000 sold under the 1929 shape and $1,486,590 to $1,264,250 under the 2008 shape — and costs $200,810 to $397,020 per $1,000,000 in the baseline. It is insurance with a known premium.
  5. Federal estate size at the date of death. The exclusion is $15,000,000 for a 2026 death and $15,480,000 for 2027, indexed after that. The household's own projection of that year determines whether the transfer tax is zero or 40% of the excess. IRS

What you get, and what you have to do for it

Written down before the figures, because the household asked for exactly that: the case for each path, stated as a rule and a duty rather than as a projection.

Live in it

You get: the intergenerational property-tax reduction for as long as you stay, the section 121 gain exclusion if you later sell after two years, and the use of the house.

You must: occupy within one year, file the exemption, and file the claim within three years or before any sale. Move out and the reduction ends at the next lien date.

Let it out

You get: the rent, and the house as a long-held asset.

You must: accept full reassessment at the transfer, which nearly doubles the tax, and accept that a later sale gets no section 121 relief.

Sell it

You get: liquidity, and almost no tax on the gain because the basis steps up at death.

You must: sell into whatever the market is paying that month, and then reinvest well enough to beat what the house would have done.

Sell, park, phase in

You get: the crash protection a staged entry buys, which is worth real money in a 1929- or 2008-shaped fall.

You must: write the rule before the money arrives, and accept a small certain cost in ordinary markets for a large uncertain gain in a bad one.

The recommendation, and the one thing that would overturn it

What the model says, as plainly as it can be said

Sell. On the base case, selling at the transfer beats living in the house by $5,056,280 even after the housing the living path supplies is counted in its favour, and beats letting it by $7,718,990. It wins on the base case, in five of the eight economic states, and in 72% of the resampled histories.

But the margin is a forecast, not a fact. It exists because the base case assumes house prices grow at 4.0% a year while equities return 7.0%. Raise house-price growth to 10.5% and the two tie. That is a move of 6.5 percentage points — inside the range of what these markets have actually done.

So the decision rule is not "sell". It is: if you believe house-price growth in this market will stay below roughly 10%, sell; if you believe it will beat that, hold and live in it; if you cannot form a view, sell, because selling is the choice that does not require you to be right.

Do not read the ranking as a recommendation yet. Selling wins on the base case by a wide margin, and that result is manufactured by the base case: at 4.0% house-price growth the house earns less than the equity market, so of course the money does better in the market. Move that one input to 10.49% and the answer flips. The honest statement is the break-even, the range it sits in, and the share of resampled histories in which holding wins — 28% of 1,200 here.

Scope. A decision aid for one household question: when the inherited house arrives, should the heir live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest? Every figure is a model output from a round modelling base — not either property's value — rounded to $10. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly as published and marked. Nothing here is legal, tax or investment advice.

Privacy. No names, streets, parcel numbers, postcodes, cities or neighbourhoods appear on this site, and no figure from the household's own records is published at any precision. The conclusions are published as break-evens, rankings and rates, which move only slightly across a tenfold range of property values — the small movement is caused by a fixed statutory cap and is tested and stated on the method page.
