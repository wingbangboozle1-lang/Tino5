Glossary

Every term this site uses that has a specific meaning in law, tax or finance. If a word is not here, the site does not rely on it.

TermWhat it means hereBasis
Assessed valueThe value the county taxes. It is not the market value: a long-held property can be assessed far below what it would sell for.derived
Factored base year valueThe assessed value a property had when it last changed ownership, increased each year by the lesser of 2% or inflation. Abbreviated FBV in the model.derived
Change in ownershipThe event that resets the assessed value to market. A sale is one; an inheritance usually is too.derived
Intergenerational exclusionThe rule that a parent-child transfer of a principal residence is not a change in ownership, provided the heir occupies within a year and claims the exemption.derived
Value capIn the intergenerational exclusion, the transferor's factored base year value plus an indexed amount. Value above it is added to the heir's assessment.derived
Prop 19The 2020 initiative that narrowed the parent-child exclusion and added the value cap from 16 February 2021.derived
Homeowners' exemptionA reduction in assessed value for an owner-occupied home, claimed by filing with the county. Filing it is one of the two conditions of the exclusion.derived
Step-up in basisThe rule that inherited property takes a basis equal to its value at the date of death, which is what makes a sale soon after the inheritance nearly tax-free.derived
Section 121The federal exclusion of up to $250,000 of gain on a main home, or $500,000 on a joint return, after two of the last five years of ownership and use.derived
Depreciation and recaptureThe deduction for wear on a rental building, and the rule that part of it is taxed again on a sale; the recapture rate is capped at 25%.derived
Net investment income taxA further 3.8% on investment income above a fixed income threshold, which is not indexed.derived
Transfer taxA county or city tax on the recorded price of a property, separate from the property tax and paid at the sale.derived
Escrow and titleThe neutral parties who hold funds and confirm the seller's title during a sale; their fees are part of the selling cost.derived
Imputed rentThe value of living in a home you own, which is real but never arrives as cash. This site reports it separately for exactly that reason.derived
LiquidityCash and securities the household can spend, as distinct from a house it would have to sell.derived
Opportunity costWhat the money would have earned if it had been used differently. It is the honest measure of holding a house, and the reason the sale path exists.derived
Break-evenThe value of one input at which two paths tie. Preferred over a forecast because it is measurable and testable.derived
Nominal and realNominal money is what the account says; real money is what it buys. All figures here are nominal, and inflation is modelled separately.derived
Sequence riskThe risk that bad returns arrive early, which hurts more than the same returns arriving late.derived
Phased entryInvesting a lump sum in instalments over time instead of all at once, to reduce the cost of investing at a peak.derived
Reinvestment riskThe risk that the moment you have to invest is a bad one. Phasing moves this risk rather than removing it.derived
PortabilityThe election that lets a surviving spouse use a deceased spouse's unused estate-tax exclusion; it requires a timely filed return.derived
DSUEThe deceased spousal unused exclusion — the amount portability transfers.derived
Like-kind exchangeA section 1031 exchange that defers tax when one investment property is swapped for another under strict timing rules.derived
Capital planThe dated list of large repairs a building will need, as opposed to routine maintenance.derived
Carrying costEverything paid to keep a building: tax, insurance, utilities, maintenance and capital.derived
ReserveMoney set aside for repairs a building will certainly need. A path without a reserve is a path with a surprise in it.derived
Tax rate areaThe county's geographic unit that determines which voter-approved debt rates apply to a parcel; it is why two neighbouring houses can have different tax rates.derived
Statutory amountA figure fixed by law, quoted exactly here rather than rounded, because rounding a legal threshold misstates it.derived
Modelling baseThe round value this site uses instead of the household's actual property value, so that published dollars cannot be traced back to a parcel.derived
Modelling baseThe round property value this site models instead of the household's real one, so that published dollars cannot be traced back to a parcel.derived
Base dependenceThe fact that one statutory amount — the intergenerational value cap — is a fixed number of dollars, so a more valuable house gets proportionally less protection and needs slightly faster growth to justify holding. The break-even therefore rises a little as value rises. A test asserts the size of that movement.derived

Scope. A decision aid for one household question: when the inherited house arrives, should the heir live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest? Every figure is a model output from a round modelling base — not either property's value — rounded to $10. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly as published and marked. Nothing here is legal, tax or investment advice.

Privacy. No names, streets, parcel numbers, postcodes, cities or neighbourhoods appear on this site, and no figure from the household's own records is published at any precision. The conclusions are published as break-evens, rankings and rates, which move only slightly across a tenfold range of property values — the small movement is caused by a fixed statutory cap and is tested and stated on the method page.
