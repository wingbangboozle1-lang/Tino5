Method, inputs and privacy

What the model does, what it assumes, how it is tested, and why publishing its dollars is safe. The last part is the one that matters most to the owner.

The question, in one sentence

When the inherited house arrives at some date between 2040 and 2050, should the heir live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest the proceeds — and does parking the proceeds before investing them change the answer?

How the model works

  • One engine. Every comparison — the eight paths, the eight states, the phased test and the Monte Carlo — runs through the same function. There is no second model to disagree with the first.
  • Money is cash. Imputed rent is real but is not cash, so it is never invested in the model. It is accumulated separately and reported as its own present value.
  • Housing is symmetric. By assumption F15 the heir lives either in the inherited house or in the building's vacant unit. No path charges them market rent, and none is given free rent the others are denied.
  • Debt is explicit. A path whose carrying cost exceeds its income borrows at the Treasury rate plus 1.5 points; the first call for cash and the total shortfall are reported.
  • Nothing is rounded until it is printed. The model carries full precision; only published figures are rounded to $10.
  • Deterministic. The Monte Carlo uses a fixed seed and a fixed draw count, so two builds produce identical bytes.

Why no household figure appears on this site

The household's own records contain a market value, a monthly rent and a sequence of assessed values. None of them is published here, at any precision. The reason is arithmetic: a rounded series of yearly assessments still identifies one parcel against an open assessor roll, and a rounded market value plus a rounded rent reproduces the owner's own brief. Rounding to $10 does not contain that — the owner's values were already multiples of ten.

Instead the model runs on declared modelling bases of $2,500,000 for the inherited house and $1,500,000 for the building. Both are round numbers that belong to no parcel.

What is safe to publish, and what is not

The desk first claimed that its conclusions were invariant to the modelling base. A test disproved that, and the reason is a feature of the law, not a defect in the model: the intergenerational value cap is a fixed number of dollars (it is the one-million-dollar amount plus the transferor's base, indexed every two years). A more valuable house therefore gets proportionally less protection, pays proportionally more tax, and needs slightly faster price growth to be worth holding.

What the tests do establish, and what this site publishes:

  • The ranking of the paths does not change between a base of one million and ten million.
  • The break-even moves by well under one percentage point across that tenfold range — it rises from about 10.41% at a one-million base to about 10.52% at ten million — and a test asserts the direction and the size.
  • The dollar gaps scale with the household's size, which is why the site leads with rates and break-evens rather than with dollars.

The consequence for the reader is stated plainly rather than hidden: a reader whose house is worth materially more than the modelling base should assume the break-even is a little higher than the figure below, and the flagged page records the range.

The tests

  • Base dependence: the ranking is identical between a one-million and a ten-million base; the break-even moves in the direction the fixed cap requires, by less than a percentage point.
  • Determinism: two builds of the model produce byte-identical output.
  • Monotonicity: a higher house-price growth rate never lowers the value of the holding path.
  • Break-evens actually cross: at the computed growth rate the two paths tie to within $10.
  • Exclusion mechanics: occupying lowers the assessment; not occupying resets to market; moving out re-enrols at market.
  • Statutory arithmetic: the estate tax, the 1% tax and the transfer taxes are computed from the statute and checked against hand-worked examples.
  • Privacy: every published page is scanned for names, streets, parcels, postcodes, cities, neighbourhoods, e-mail and phone shapes, and for any unrounded money.
  • Sources: every external host is on the official allowlist; every data row links one or says why none exists.

Rebuilding after an input change

The owner's requirement — rebuild the model and the site after any input change and see whether the recommendation moved — is one command. The build regenerates every page from the model, and the break-even table is printed at the end of the run so a change is visible without reading the site.

python3 desk-ops/_src/model.py    # writes model.json and prints the break-evens
python3 desk-ops/_src/build.py    # rewrites every page
python3 desk-ops/_src/verify.py   # the publication contract
python3 -m unittest discover -s desk-ops/_src -p "test_*.py"

What this is not

Not legal, tax or investment advice. Not a forecast. Not a valuation. The break-evens are the useful part, and they are only as good as the ranges above — which is why the ranges are published next to them.

Scope. A decision aid for one household question: when the inherited house arrives, should the heir live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest? Every figure is a model output from a round modelling base — not either property's value — rounded to $10. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly as published and marked. Nothing here is legal, tax or investment advice.

Privacy. No names, streets, parcel numbers, postcodes, cities or neighbourhoods appear on this site, and no figure from the household's own records is published at any precision. The conclusions are published as break-evens, rankings and rates, which move only slightly across a tenfold range of property values — the small movement is caused by a fixed statutory cap and is tested and stated on the method page.
