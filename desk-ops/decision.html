The decision, compared on one footing

Eight paths, not three. Every path is run at the same transfer year and the same horizon, with the same tax rules, the same debt mechanics, the same occupancy conditions, the same capital costs and the same liquidity measure. A path that cannot pay its own carrying cost borrows and shows the debt.

The comparison that matters

At a 2045 transfer and a 20-year horizon the cash ranking is Sell at the transfer ($19,139,330), then Sell, park in Treasuries, phase in ($18,637,300), then Let it, then sell ($14,072,680). Counted with the housing the path supplies, Live in it rises from $4,565,360 to $14,083,050 but still does not lead. The gap between first and last is $14,573,970 in cash — and it is produced almost entirely by the gap between the assumed house-price growth (4.0%) and the assumed equity return (7.0%).

Why mixed paths beat both pure choices in some states

"Live in it, then sell" is not a compromise for its own sake. It is the only path that collects two reliefs: the intergenerational exclusion while the heir occupies, and the section 121 home-sale exclusion on the gain when they sell after two years of ownership and use. The pure letting path collects neither on a later sale, and the pure living path never converts the house into money.

RTC 63.2(a)(1)(B)-(C) ends the exclusion when the heir stops occupying and re-enrols the base year value at the next lien date. IRS Topic 701 requires two of the last five years of ownership and use for the section 121 exclusion. The mixed path is the only one where both are ever available — and the exclusion's removal on sale is exactly why the later sale is taxed, which the model computes rather than ignores.

Liquidity: what the heir can actually spend

The living and letting paths tie up most of the estate in one illiquid asset. On the base case the living path ends with $0 of liquid assets against $19,139,330 for the sale. That is the real cost of holding: not the expected return, but the option the heir gives up.

The distribution, not the point

1,200 resampled 20-year histories, fixed seed 20261115, drawing annual equity returns around a 7.0% mean and house-price growth around 4.0%. Each bar is one sixteenth of the outcomes; red bars are histories in which letting the house beat selling it.

Left to right: worst to best for the holding path. Percentiles of the let-minus-sell gap: 5th $-52,907,167, 25th $-18,643,424, median $-6,221,012, 75th $745,225, 95th $8,165,036. Holding wins in 28% of histories.

How to read this. A single projection says "sell wins by $-7,718,990" and invites a decision. The distribution says the same thing without the false precision: the median favours selling, the spread is larger than the median, and holding still wins in 28% of the histories drawn. A decision that has to survive bad luck should be chosen against the spread, not the median.

Reinvestment risk sits beside every staging result. The distribution assumes the sale proceeds are invested at the equity mean from the first day. The phased-entry page tests that assumption against two real drawdowns; the answer is that phasing is cheap insurance in a crash and an expensive one in a boom. assumption Neither the draw nor the mean is a forecast.
What would change the ranking. House-price growth above 10.49%; equities below 4.83%; a rent yield above 6.8% of value; or the heir valuing the housing at more than 5.0% of the house's value a year. Each is a single input, and each is listed in the range register.

Scope. A decision aid for one household question: when the inherited house arrives, should the heir live in it, let it out, or sell it and invest? Every figure is a model output from a round modelling base — not either property's value — rounded to $10. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly as published and marked. Nothing here is legal, tax or investment advice.

Privacy. No names, streets, parcel numbers, postcodes, cities or neighbourhoods appear on this site, and no figure from the household's own records is published at any precision. The conclusions are published as break-evens, rankings and rates, which move only slightly across a tenfold range of property values — the small movement is caused by a fixed statutory cap and is tested and stated on the method page.
