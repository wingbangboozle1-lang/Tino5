This is the piece the earlier generations of this project left out, and it is why the occupy rows are not as far behind as a raw yield comparison suggests: housing is a service the already-owned house provides, and it can only be provided once. Ignoring it flatters selling; crediting it to every path is simply wrong.

The sequencing error to avoid. Letting the inherited house before the exclusion is claimed is not a neutral choice: it is evidence against principal-residence use, and the exclusion decides a larger number than the rent does. Conversely, do not hand the already-owned rental over to the heir while it is let - a rental has no family-home exclusion, so the transfer resets it to market value permanently.

Recommendation, and the reason for it

  1. Claim and keep the exclusion, and decide the rest later. The exclusion is cheap to claim and irreplaceable to recover; the sell-versus-hold question does not have to be answered in the year of the death. Occupying for a decade and then selling ends $2,974,890 below selling at once at a 2045 transfer - the price of keeping both futures open, and the only path that finishes first when history is replayed at us on the black swans page.
  2. Do not plan on renting it out. It forfeits the exclusion, and the rent that would make up for that is about $13,550 a month at a 2045 transfer - far above anything the local market is reported to pay for a house at this price.
  3. If the answer is to sell, sell into strength, not on a deadline. The forced-sale-at-the-trough regime on the black swans page is the only stress that changes the ranking more than the assumptions do.
  4. Let the vacant unit at the other house, if you are not living in it. It is worth about $3,706,130 by 2076, which is more than the whole carrying-cost table of the inherited house, and it does not depend on the inheritance at all.
  5. Get the estate-size number. Whether 40% is payable on anything is set by the cash, not by the house, and no other question in this file can move the answer as far.
