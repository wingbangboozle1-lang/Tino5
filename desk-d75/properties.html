Why the asymmetry matters. The inherited house's advantage is a conditional low tax base that the heir can lose by not living there. The already-owned house's advantage is an unconditional low tax base that is destroyed by handing it over while it is let, and protected by doing nothing. The two houses therefore point at opposite behaviours: rush to occupy one, do not rush to transfer the other.

Verification result. Each year of the inherited house's assessed-value history that you supplied was divided by the year before and compared with the published factor for that roll year. Every year matched to within a few dollars, including the years the factor was below the cap (1.525% and 1.036%), which is what a true Proposition 13 base does and what a reassessed property would not show. The already-owned house's history matches in every year except one, where the step is about $45 short of 2%. Neither history is published on this desk, at any rounding, because a rounded series can be matched to one parcel in the open roll - see the privacy rule.

The adjusted amount is $1,044,586 exact for transfers between 16 February 2025 and 15 February 2027, and is re-indexed the following February; the figures in the last column are this desk's projection of that indexation (D4), not published amounts. Read the last two columns together. The exclusion does not preserve the old base; it subtracts the adjusted amount from the market value. Because this house is worth far more than the base plus that amount, most of its value is reassessed anyway, and what the heir actually saves each year is about the amount in the final column times the rate - roughly $26,700 a year at a 2045 transfer, on top of the difference the second and third columns show. An earlier generation of this project treated the exclusion as shielding the whole gap, which is the error the referee tree logged and this page corrects.
