Clean dashboard for deciding what to do with an inherited house. Property A passes through a trust around 2040–2050; Property B is already owned outright. The two are analysed separately because one is inherited and the other is not. Every table row cites an official source or is marked unverified; money is rounded to the nearest $10.

What would change the answer (read first)

The decision is not determined by today's headline price. It flips on three break-evens:

  • Gross rental yield on Property A vs. the cost of carrying it (tax, insurance, maintenance, water/electric). If net rent after carrying costs exceeds the after-tax portfolio return, keeping wins.
  • Price growth vs. portfolio return. If the house appreciates faster than a diversified portfolio (after tax), keeping compounds better.
  • Whether the heir moves in within one year of transfer. That is the switch between Prop 19's capped reassessment (child occupies) and full market reassessment (child does not occupy). [source]
These break-evens are shown on the Executive Summary and the Scenarios page, with what-if controls so you can test them yourself.

Executive summary

  • Property A's inheritance is a change in ownership at the date of death, not at distribution, for property held in a trust. [source: BOE Proposition 19]
  • Property B is owned already and is not part of the inheritance; its rent and costs are shown only where they change the Property A comparison. Owner-supplied: two of its three units are let.
  • Selling is the highest-value option at the model's return, but it is the option most exposed to how the proceeds are invested and to a market crash just after the sale. See the crash-entry replay.
  • Every money figure is rounded to the nearest $10. Owner figures are marked owner-supplied and unverified; the verified cells stay blank.
