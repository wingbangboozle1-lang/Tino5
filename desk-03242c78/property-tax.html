Property tax and assessment: three rules, one bill

California property tax is not one tax. It is a base rate, a cap on how fast the assessed value can grow, and a set of local add-ons that differ street by street. Two of those are statewide and exact. The third is local, published by tax rate area, and is the reason this desk will not state a single tax rate for either building.

Rule one: one percent, plus local add-ons

Proposition 13 sets the general levy at one percent of assessed value. Everything above that — county bonds, school bonds, city measures, district assessments — is approved locally and differs by tax rate area. A county has hundreds of them. The Santa Clara County assessor's own booklet illustrates a typical area at 1.1488 per $100 and cautions that the illustration is typical, not universal. Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor

Rule two: assessed value grows at most 2% a year

The factored base year value rises each year by the change in the California consumer price index, but never more than 2 percent. In a year of high inflation the cap binds; in a year of deflation the value does not fall. This is why a building bought decades ago carries an assessed value far below its market value, and why the household's Property B is so cheap to hold. Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor

Rule three: a change of ownership resets the value

When a property changes ownership, it is reassessed at market value and the new figure becomes the base for the 2 percent cap. An inheritance is a change of ownership. The exception is the parent-child exclusion under Proposition 19, which has its own page. A transfer into a revocable trust by the owner is not a change of ownership; a distribution out of the trust to a beneficiary at death is. California Legislature

The parcel's own tax rate area was not obtained. A buyer of Property A would get the actual rate from the title company before closing; the household should get it from its own tax bill now.

This is the cost of the exclusion decision stated as an annual bill rather than as a one-time benefit. It is what makes the occupancy question a real financial decision instead of a formality.

Two dates that cost money if missed

  • 15 February — the homeowners' exemption claim for the year. A late claim may be accepted through 10 December at a penalty. Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor
  • 2 July to 15 September — the window to appeal an assessed value. For a supplemental or escape assessment, 60 days from the notice. An appeal is free and does not require an attorney. Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor
Both dates are on the deadline timeline with the rest of the filing calendar, in the order they occur across a transfer year.
