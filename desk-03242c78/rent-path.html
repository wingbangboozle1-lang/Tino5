Rent Property A out

Let the house and move into B's third unit. This is the only path that collects rent from both properties while housing the household for free — and it is the worst of the three pure choices in every regime tested.

The central number here is unverified. The rent figure is a floor, not an estimate. No independent comparables were obtained for Property A, so the model uses $7,500 a month, inside a $6,000 to $9,000 band. If actual rent is at the top of the band the path improves by about a modest amount over twenty years — still not enough to pass selling. If it is at the bottom, the path is worse than shown.

The three reasons this path loses

  1. The yield is below the risk-free rate. A house worth $3,100,000 that rents for $90,000 a year yields 2.90% gross. After tax, insurance, water and maintenance it yields roughly -0.55%. A two-year Treasury yielded 4.88% on 13 November 2026. The house is not an income asset at this price; it is a price-growth bet with a carrying cost.
  2. No exclusion, no step-up. The rental produces no Proposition 19 relief and forfeits section 121, and the depreciation it does take is recaptured at 25 percent on sale.
  3. It ties up the household's housing decision. The household lives in B's third unit, which is fine, but the arrangement couples two buildings that were meant to be separate decisions.
