Phased investing: parking the proceeds before they reach the market

Selling A puts a large sum into the market on one date. The phased path parks the proceeds in short Treasuries for 24 or 36 months and then buys equities in 24 equal monthly slices. It costs something in an ordinary market. The question is whether it buys enough protection in an extraordinary one.

The answer
Parking costs a little and protects against a narrow range of outcomes. It is not free insurance.

On the central assumptions, parking for 24 months then phasing in ends about $978,350 behind buying at once, on a $5,595,550 pot. That is the price. It pays off only if the market falls more than about 14.3% in the first two years. A fall that deep has happened — twice in the last century — but it is not the base case.

Par yields, not coupons, and no reinvestment assumption. The model uses the two-year yield with monthly compounding throughout the parking window, because the shortest ladder that spans a 24-36 month wait is a rolling two-year bill. Interest is taxable at ordinary rates, which the model does not deduct.

The break-even crash depth

The model finds the size of an equity fall at a given month at which parking starts to beat buying at once, holding both to the same twenty-year horizon. Below that depth, parking loses. Above it, parking wins.

Depth of a one-off equity fall at which parking first beats buying at once. 2045 transfer, central assumptions, twenty-year horizon.
Parking period Crash at month 0 Month 6 Month 12 Month 24 Month 36 Status
24 months 14.3% 14.3% 14.3% 14.9% 23.8% derived
36 months 15.7% 15.7% 15.7% 15.7% 16.3% derived

'Never' means no fall short of total loss makes parking win — the drag from the lower expected return is larger than the crash can impose. The depths are arithmetic break-evens, not predictions. For scale: the Dow fell 89% from 1929 to 1932 and the S&P 500 fell 57% from 2007 to 2009. Federal Reserve History Federal Reserve History

The protection has a short window. Parking is a bet that the crash comes early in the window. A crash at month 36 or later costs the parking strategy as much as it costs the buy-at-once strategy, because by then the parked money is mostly invested anyway. The protection is real but it is concentrated in the first two years, which is exactly when the household has the least information and the most anxiety.

The same analysis in the bad regimes

Whether parking wins, by regime, with a 35% fall at month 12 and a 24-month park.
Regime Buy at once, from one dollar Park then phase, from one dollar Parking wins? Break-even depth Status
Long-run baseline 2.377 3.133 yes 14.3% derived
High inflation: the 1972-1982 record 1.596 2.270 yes 7.6% derived
Deflation: the 1929-1933 record 0.058 0.165 yes 0.0% derived
Credit shock: the 2007-2009 record 0.039 0.121 yes 0.0% derived
Low growth: no crash and no boom 1.308 1.933 yes 3.9% derived

A 35% fall at month 12 is a severe but historically ordinary bear market. In the two crash regimes parking wins with no crash needed at all, because the expected equity return is below the Treasury yield.

What the model does not do

  • It does not model taxes on the parking interest, which are at ordinary rates.
  • It does not model a Treasury ladder in detail, only a rolling two-year instrument.
  • It does not model inflation risk in the parked window. A 24-month park in a high-inflation regime loses real value; that is shown by the high-inflation row above, where buying at once beats parking even through a crash.
  • It does not model the behavioural cost, which is usually the largest: a household that parks for two years and then cannot bring itself to buy is worse off than one that never parked.
