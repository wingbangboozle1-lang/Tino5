Live in it, rent it out, or sell it?

Two California homes and one future inheritance, compared on the same dates, the same horizon and the same tax treatment. Property A is a house in Santa Clara County, expected through a trust between 2040 and 2050. Property B is a three-unit building in San Francisco County, already owned: two units are let and the owner may take the third without losing that rent.

The answer, on the central assumptions
Selling A at the transfer date ranks first — but the margin over keeping it is inside the error bars.

Ranked at a 2045 transfer over twenty years: sell at once $29,631,180, live in A $22,963,440, let A out $21,170,610. The sell advantage over living in A is $6,667,740 over twenty years, about $333,390 a year. That is a real difference, but it turns on house price growth staying below about 6.1% a year — see what would change the answer.

The more stable finding: letting A out is the weakest of the three pure choices at every transfer date under the baseline, and it loses to living in A in every regime tested. At $3,100,000 of value against roughly $90,000 a year of rent, a house here yields under 3% gross. The building cannot pay for itself at these prices, and the tax code rewards owning it as a home, not as a rental.

Property B is not part of that comparison and must not be. It is already owned. Moving into B does not produce the same gain as inheriting A, because a change of residence inside a building the household already owns does not reset its assessed value. The two are separated on why they are not comparable.

Reinvestment risk, stated before the numbers. Every headline on this desk is a portfolio outcome, and every portfolio outcome here depends on what happens to the money after the sale. Selling A and buying equities converts a building the household can touch into a price series it cannot control. That risk sits beside each headline below, not at the end of the document.

The recommendation, stated as a sequence

  1. Sell A at the transfer date. Basis resets to market value at death, so a sale in the same tax year as the inheritance produces no meaningful capital gain. Waiting converts a tax-free step-up into a taxable gain. See income tax and basis.
  2. Do not occupy A to capture the Proposition 19 exclusion unless the household genuinely intends to live there for years. The exclusion requires occupancy as a principal residence within one year and an exemption claim within one year; taking it and then leaving can cost the whole benefit back. See Proposition 19.
  3. Write the investment policy before the money arrives. The single largest controllable risk is what the household does in the first eighteen months after a sale it did not plan for. See policy for the proceeds.
  4. Move into B's third unit, or stay put, before touching A. That decision is free, reversible and independent of the inheritance.
  5. Do not sell B. It carries a pre-1978-style assessed base in practice and a rent roll that funds the household's own housing. Selling it would convert sheltered cash flow into a taxable gain.

What this desk will not tell you

It will not tell you what A is worth. The household's figure of $3,100,000 is an anchor supplied by the owner, not an appraisal, and it is unverified. It will not tell you what A would rent for: no independent comparables were obtained, so the rent figure is a labelled assumption and every letting path is a floor, not an estimate. It will not price the trust, because the trust instrument has not been read. Those gaps are listed, with the action that closes each one, on questions and gaps.
