Section 121: what it is worth, and what forfeits it

The section 121 exclusion lets a homeowner exclude up to $250,000 of gain on a sale of a principal residence, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. It requires two of the last five years of ownership and use. For this household it is worth less than it looks, because the step-up at death already gives a nearly tax-free sale — but it is the reason a live-then-sell path is not as bad as it would otherwise be.

The rule, quoted

The section 121 conditions, from the IRS publication for sellers of a home.
Condition Requirement How it applies here Status
Amount excluded $250,000 of gain for a single filer, $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Internal Revenue Service The household's filing status is not in the file, so the model uses the single amount throughout. A joint return would double it.
Ownership test Owned the home for at least two of the five years ending on the sale date. Internal Revenue Service Satisfied by inheritance at death: the heir owns from the date of death.
Use test Lived in the home as a principal residence for at least two of the five years ending on the sale date. Internal Revenue Service This is the binding constraint. It is also the Proposition 19 condition.
Frequency Not excluded gain from another home sale in the two years before this one. Internal Revenue Service Unknown. Question for the CPA.
Exchange disqualification Gain is not excludable if the home was acquired in a section 1031 like-kind exchange within the past five years. Internal Revenue Service Not applicable to an inheritance, but relevant if A is exchanged first.

What it is worth in this model

The live-then-sell paths against selling at the transfer date, at a 2045 transfer.
Path Gain before exclusion Excluded Taxable gain Tax saved by 121 Net proceeds Status
Sell at transfer $0 $0 $0 $0 $5,595,550 derived
Live 5 years, then sell $254,510 $250,000 $436,010 $92,750 $6,484,010 derived
Live 10 years, then sell $1,933,340 $250,000 $1,683,340 $92,750 $7,268,580 derived
Let 10 years, then sell $4,109,880 $0 $2,809,570 $0 $6,850,750 derived

The combined marginal rate on a large gain is 37.1%, so taxable gain is recovered from tax by dividing. Gain before exclusion is the taxable gain plus the excluded amount; recapture is excluded from these columns because it is taxed separately at 25%. The exclusion is capped at $250,000, which is why living longer does not shield more.

Do not overstate the exclusion. The exclusion is smaller than the gain in every live-then-sell path. At a 2045 transfer, five years of ownership produces a gain far above $250,000, so the exclusion covers only part of it and the rest is taxed at the combined rate. Section 121 is not a reason to live in the house; it is a reason that living in it for a few years is not financially irrational.

The partial exclusion, and when it applies

The IRS allows a reduced exclusion when a sale is forced by a change in employment, a change in health, or an unforeseeable event, and when the two-year use test is not met. The reduced amount is proportional to the time the tests were met. Internal Revenue Service

The model does not compute the partial exclusion. It applies the full $250,000 to every live-then-sell path that holds for at least two years, which is the conservative direction: a partial exclusion would be smaller. A household forced to sell earlier would do worse than modelled, not better.
