Workbook: every input, its status, and what happens if it moves

This is the page to argue with. Every number the model uses appears once, with where it came from, and with a note on what it would take to replace it with something better. Anything marked assumption is a placeholder for a fact the household does not yet have.

What the model does not include

  • Tax on portfolio income. Dividends, interest and realised gains inside the portfolio are taxed annually. The model uses a gross return.
  • Fees. An adviser charging 1 percent a year on a portfolio of this size would consume about a fifth of the assumed real return.
  • Vacancy longer than the allowance. A 5 percent vacancy allowance on A; a longer vacancy in a soft rental market would hurt.
  • Rent control and eviction costs. A three-unit building in San Francisco is subject to the rent ordinance. Not modelled.
  • Long-term care and health costs. The largest financial risk in most households this age, and entirely outside this decision.
  • The trust. No term of the trust instrument was read. The desk assumes A passes outright with basis reset.
Every omission above pushes in the same direction: they make the sell-and-invest path look better than it is. Presenting them plainly is the only honest way to publish a recommendation that favours that path.

This is a planning document, not advice. It is prepared for review by a licensed California attorney and a CPA before any step is taken. No legal, tax or investment advice is given or implied. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly and marked; every other figure is an assumption, an owner-supplied anchor, or arithmetic on those.

Nothing here identifies a person, a street address, an assessor's parcel number, or a ZIP code. Owner-supplied figures are approximate and unverified. Money is rounded to the nearest $10 except where a statute or official table states an exact amount.
