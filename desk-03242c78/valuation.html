Date-of-death valuation

One number governs three separate taxes and one non-tax question, and it has to be established in the worst possible circumstances: the fair market value of Property A on the date the owner dies. It becomes the income-tax basis automatically, it is reported on the estate tax return, and it is the starting point for the property tax reassessment.

Why this one number matters so much
It sets the basis, the estate value, and the fairness of both.

A valuation set too low understates basis, so the heir pays more income tax on a later sale. Set too high, it overstates the estate and could produce an estate tax liability that is not owed. There is no single number that is optimal for both; there is only a number that is defensible.

What the number is used for

Four uses of one date-of-death value, and what happens if it is wrong.
Use What it does If it is too low If it is too high Status
Income-tax basis Sets the gain on any later sale; the step-up is automatic and requires no election. Internal Revenue Service The heir pays capital gain tax on value that existed at death. Nothing; the heir benefits, but the number will not survive an audit.
Estate tax value Included in the gross estate and compared with the exclusion. Internal Revenue Service Understates the estate. Low risk if the estate is far below the threshold. Overstates the estate and may create tax that is not owed.
Consistent-basis report Reported to the IRS and to the heir on Form 8971, and must match what the heir uses. Internal Revenue Service A mismatch between the estate return and the heir's return invites inquiry. Same mismatch, in the other direction.
Property tax reassessment The assessor's own value, which is separately determined and may differ. Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor — —

How to establish it after the fact

Three ways to value a house as of a past date, in order of how well they hold up.
Method What it produces When to use it Cost and timing Status
Retrospective appraisal by a licensed appraiser An opinion of value as of the date of death, using comparable sales from that period and the property's condition then. Always, and commissioned as soon as possible after death. Weeks to months. The longer the delay, the weaker the opinion. derived
Estate's own appraisal at the time A contemporaneous valuation, prepared for the estate return. When an appraisal is required for the return, which is usual for a property of this value. Commissioned within the first months. derived
Assessor's transfer value The county's assessed value after the change of ownership. As a cross-check, never as the basis figure. Arrives on the supplemental bill. Often differs from market value materially. derived
Delay destroys evidence. A valuation obtained years after the death is a reconstruction. It relies on comparable sales from a past market and on somebody's memory of the property's condition. If the heir intends to sell within the same tax year, the sale price itself is the best evidence of value — which is one more argument for deciding quickly, and one reason the desk models the sale in the year of death first.

What the model does with it

The model assumes the date-of-death value equals the market value for that year, so the basis equals the sale price only if the sale happens immediately. Any gap between the appraised value and the actual sale price creates gain or loss, and the model does not attempt to predict that gap. At a 2045 transfer under central assumptions the value is $5,959,750.

The household's current anchor of $3,100,000 is used only to establish the starting point for growth. It is not a date-of-death value and must never be reported as one on any return or in any filing.
