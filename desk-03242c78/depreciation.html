Depreciation and recapture: the deduction that comes back

Letting a house out allows a deduction for depreciation — the building wearing out. It is a real deduction and it does reduce tax while the house is rented. It is also a loan. On sale, the depreciation taken is recaptured at a higher rate than the capital gain, and the household pays it back whether or not the deduction was actually claimed.

The rule

Residential rental real property placed in service after 1986 is depreciated under the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System over 27.5 years, straight line, using the mid-month convention. Land is never depreciable; only the building is. On sale, gain attributable to depreciation is unrecaptured section 1250 gain and is taxed at a maximum rate of 25 percent before any capital gain rate applies. Nominal dollars of the sale year. The letting row also loses the section 121 exclusion entirely, because the use test fails: the household did not live there two of the last five years.

The deduction is a loan. Recapture is the part of the rental case that is most often overlooked. A household that lets the house out for ten years takes roughly $1,300,310 of depreciation, and on sale pays about $325,080 of tax on it at 25%, before any capital gain. That is money that was borrowed, not money that was earned, and it is due in the year of sale whether or not the household is ready.

What would change this page

  • An appraisal splitting land from building. The 60 percent building share is the largest single assumption in the depreciation figures.
  • Capital improvements. Improvements are depreciated separately over their own recovery period and add to recapture. None are assumed.
  • A cost segregation study. For a house of this vintage the benefit is usually small and the cost often exceeds it; it is not modelled.
