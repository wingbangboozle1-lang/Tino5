Five-year building plan for both properties

A house and a three-unit building both need money spent on them, and the money arrives in lumps. The projection uses a smooth reserve — a percentage of value every year — because a smooth line is easier to compare. The real spending is not smooth, and this page says what it probably looks like.

This page is entirely assumption. Every figure on this page is an assumption. No contractor has been asked, no inspection has been done, and no records of past work were supplied. The percentages are the reserve convention, not a condition assessment. A building inspection and three quotes for the largest items would replace this page entirely.

These are planning placeholders, not quotes. The five-year total for Property A is about 4.8% of its value and for Property B about 11.1%; both are inside the 20-year reserve accumulations above, which is the point of holding a reserve.

Why this matters to the decision

Maintenance is one of the few costs that differs sharply between paths. A house sold at the transfer date needs no reserve for the following twenty years. A house kept for twenty years needs a new roof, a sewer lateral and a heating system, and the buyer will discount the price for any of them left undone. The model captures the average of that with the reserve line; the lumpiness is what actually breaks a household's cash flow.

The practical rule: fund the reserve whether or not the house is kept. A house being sold still needs to be in saleable condition, and the cost of preparing it for sale is the first thing buyers negotiate. The reserve is not a cost of keeping; it is a cost of owning anything.
