Rents, and why every letting figure here is a floor

The desk has no independent rent estimate for Property A. Not a comparable listing, not an appraiser's opinion, not the federal government's fair market rent for the area — nothing that would meet the standard the rest of this desk is held to. So the rent figure is labelled an assumption, and every letting path is described as a floor rather than an estimate.

What is used, and what it is not
A labelled assumption of $7,500 a month, inside a $6,000 to $9,000 band.

It is not a market rent. It has not been checked against a single comparable. It is a planning figure chosen so that the letting case is tested fairly: at the top of the band, letting A out still does not beat selling at the central house growth assumption. If the true rent is at the bottom of the band, the case against letting is stronger than shown.

The rent floor, and what a real one would take

Three ways to establish a defensible rent for Property A, in order of how much they would be worth. None has been done.
Method What it produces What it costs Status Status
Three written rental comparables A defensible market rent for a house of this size and condition in this submarket, from recently let units. A local property manager will usually provide them free in exchange for a management conversation. not obtained
HUD Fair Market Rent for the area The 40th-percentile gross rent for standard-quality units in the metropolitan area, published annually. A published government figure, but for the whole area and not for this house. U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development Free. available, not used
Professional rental appraisal An opinion of rent from a licensed appraiser, usable in a dispute. A few hundred dollars. not obtained

The HUD figure is published and free and would at least bound the assumption. It is deliberately not used as the rent here, because an area-wide 40th-percentile rent for standard-quality units is not a market rent for a specific house, and using it would give the letting case a false authority.

Property B's rent is a different matter

Property B has tenants and leases. Its rent is not an assumption; it is a contract. The desk uses the owner's figure of $7,150 a month for two units, which is roughly $3,580 per unit. That figure should be checked against the leases themselves, together with the tenancy start dates, the deposit amounts and any rent-control registration. It has not been.

What the model assumes about Property B's rent, and what would verify or change it.
Assumption Why it matters How to verify Status
Both let units stay let for twenty years A vacancy of even six months on one unit is a five-figure loss at this rent. Tenancy history, plus the local rental market's vacancy rate. derived
Rent can be raised with inflation Under the local rent ordinance, annual increases are capped and require notice. The model grows rent at the general inflation rate, which may exceed the cap. The rent ordinance's current allowable increase, and each unit's registration and lawful rent. derived
The third unit can be occupied without losing the other two rents This is the household's stated position and it is the basis of every path that houses the household in B. The leases and the rent ordinance's owner-occupancy provisions. derived
No owner move-in eviction is needed If the third unit is currently let, moving in may require an eviction, which is expensive, slow and regulated. Whether the third unit is vacant. The owner's description implies it is. derived
The cap that is not modelled. Rent control is the largest unmodelled risk in Property B. A three-unit building in San Francisco falls under the rent ordinance; annual increases are limited, and eviction for owner move-in is regulated and can require relocation payments. The model grows B's rent with general inflation, which is optimistic if the cap binds. On B, this makes the household's cash flow look better than it may be — the opposite direction from the omissions listed in the workbook.
Both directions of bias are now stated: the omissions listed on the workbook make sell-and-invest look better, and this one makes keeping B look better. They are not equal in size, and the desk does not claim they cancel. What it claims is that both are disclosed, which is what a reader needs in order to decide how much to trust the ranking.

This is a planning document, not advice. It is prepared for review by a licensed California attorney and a CPA before any step is taken. No legal, tax or investment advice is given or implied. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly and marked; every other figure is an assumption, an owner-supplied anchor, or arithmetic on those.

Nothing here identifies a person, a street address, an assessor's parcel number, or a ZIP code. Owner-supplied figures are approximate and unverified. Money is rounded to the nearest $10 except where a statute or official table states an exact amount.
