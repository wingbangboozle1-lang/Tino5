A policy for the proceeds, written before the money arrives

The single largest controllable risk in this decision is not the tax, the commission or the rent. It is what the household does with a large, sudden sum of money while grieving and under deadline pressure. A written policy, adopted in advance, is the only reliable defence.

The point of writing it down first
A policy written after the money arrives is a rationalisation.

A document adopted before the sale, while the household is calm and the amount is hypothetical, is the only version that will bind when the money is real. It should state the asset mix, the phasing schedule, the spending rule and the triggers for changing course — and it should say who is allowed to override it, and how.

Terms the policy should fix in advance

Each term below is a decision that is much cheaper to make now than after a sale.
Term Why it must be decided in advance A defensible starting point Status
How much stays in cash The household will want more cash than it needs. Deciding the amount first prevents the whole sum becoming cash by default. One to two years of household spending, plus the reserve for both buildings. derived
Whether to phase in, and over how long The phased path costs a little in an ordinary market and protects against an early crash. See phased investing. Park 24 months in Treasuries, then buy in 24 equal monthly slices. The model quantifies the cost: about $978,350 at a 2045 transfer. derived
The asset mix A single large sum invites a single large position. The mix should be set by target percentages, not by conviction. A diversified mix appropriate to the household's age and other income. This desk does not recommend specific allocations. derived
The spending rule Liquidity changes behaviour. A rule such as a fixed percentage of the portfolio each year prevents the portfolio being consumed by its own accessibility. A percentage of the trailing average balance, set low enough that a bad decade does not force a sale. derived
Review triggers Annual review is too slow for a crash and too fast for nothing. Annual review, plus a scheduled review after any fall greater than 20 percent, with the standing instruction to do nothing on the day of the fall. derived
Who may override the policy Someone will want to. Name them, and require a written reason. No unilateral override. A change requires both spouses and a written note. derived
The adviser question Whether to use an adviser, and on what fee basis, is easier to decide before the money exists. If an adviser is used, a fee-only arrangement, and a fee low enough that it does not consume the assumed real return. One percent a year on this sum is a five- or six-figure deduction over twenty years. derived
Costs and taxes can reverse the answer. The model's equity return is gross of tax and fees. A taxable portfolio of this size in a high bracket, with an adviser charging one percent, might keep a little over four percent of a six and a half percent gross return. At that net return, living in Property A wins. The policy's costs are not a detail; they can reverse the recommendation.

What to do in the first thirty days after the sale

  1. Put the proceeds in a Treasury money market or short Treasury ladder the same week. Not a bank deposit above insured limits, and not an investment account with a pending allocation.
  2. Do not make any other financial decision for thirty days. No new property, no gifts, no adviser contract.
  3. Confirm the tax set-aside. Even a near-zero-gain sale has costs and state consequences; the CPA should confirm the amount before any of it is committed.
  4. Execute the policy's first tranche. The point of writing the policy in advance is that this step requires no further judgement.
This page deliberately recommends no specific security, allocation or adviser. It sets out the decisions a policy must contain and the arithmetic that makes each one consequential. Any actual allocation is a matter for a licensed adviser acting on the household's full circumstances.

This is a planning document, not advice. It is prepared for review by a licensed California attorney and a CPA before any step is taken. No legal, tax or investment advice is given or implied. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly and marked; every other figure is an assumption, an owner-supplied anchor, or arithmetic on those.

Nothing here identifies a person, a street address, an assessor's parcel number, or a ZIP code. Owner-supplied figures are approximate and unverified. Money is rounded to the nearest $10 except where a statute or official table states an exact amount.
