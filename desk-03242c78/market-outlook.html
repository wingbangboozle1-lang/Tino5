What the long record shows

Rather than assume a growth rate and defend it, the desk measures the record. Everything on this page is computed from published series: the BLS consumer price index from 1913, and the Federal Reserve's own histories of the two worst market episodes of the last century.

Two drawdowns, measured

The two episodes used as stress regimes, with the official figures.
Episode Peak to trough Recovery Source Status
1929 to 1932, equities The Dow fell from 381.17 on 3 September 1929 to 41.22 on 8 July 1932, -89%. The index did not regain its 1929 high until 23 November 1954 — twenty-five years. Federal Reserve History
2007 to 2009, equities The S&P 500 fell -57% from its October 2007 peak to its March 2009 trough. Recovered within the following decade. Federal Reserve History
2006 to 2009, house prices Home prices fell about -30% from mid-2006 to mid-2009. Varies by market; California recovered strongly and then some. Federal Reserve History
2007 to 2009, the economy Real GDP fell 4.3% peak to trough; unemployment peaked at 10% in October 2009. The recession ended in June 2009. Federal Reserve History
Twenty years was not long enough once. The 1929 record is the reason a twenty-year horizon is not a guarantee. An investor who put money in at the 1929 peak and held did not break even in nominal terms until 1954. The household's twenty-year window from 2045 is the same length. Anyone who says 'twenty years is long enough' has not read this table.

The anchors are what gives the regimes their authority: the inflation rates are measured, the drawdowns are measured, and only the annual house and equity rates within each regime are assumptions.

The honest conclusion from this record is that the future will not look like the average. It will look like one of the episodes, and which one cannot be known. That is why the desk publishes a distribution and not a point estimate, and why the recommendation is stated as a starting point rather than an answer.

This is a planning document, not advice. It is prepared for review by a licensed California attorney and a CPA before any step is taken. No legal, tax or investment advice is given or implied. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly and marked; every other figure is an assumption, an owner-supplied anchor, or arithmetic on those.

Nothing here identifies a person, a street address, an assessor's parcel number, or a ZIP code. Owner-supplied figures are approximate and unverified. Money is rounded to the nearest $10 except where a statute or official table states an exact amount.
