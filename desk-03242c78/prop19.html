Proposition 19: the exclusion that pays for occupancy

Proposition 19 lets a child keep a parent's assessed value when a family home is inherited, but only up to a limit and only if the child actually moves in. Both conditions are strict, both have deadlines, and failing either one is expensive. This page quotes the statute and computes what the exclusion is worth to this household.

The two conditions, in the statute's words
Move in within one year. Claim the exemption within one year. Keep living there.

The exclusion applies to the transferor's principal residence when that residence becomes the transferee's principal residence. The transferee must file for the homeowners' or disabled veterans' exemption within one year of the transfer. If the transferee later stops being eligible, the exclusion is removed and the property is reassessed as of that date. It is not a one-time certificate; it is a continuing condition.

The statute, quoted

Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2, as published by the Legislature. Quoted verbatim; the paragraph breaks are the desk's.

“Notwithstanding subdivision (a), the purchase or transfer of a family home of the transferor shall not be a ‘change in ownership’ for purposes of this chapter, if the transferee or transferees, as applicable, file for the homeowners’ exemption or disabled veterans’ exemption, as applicable, within one year of the transfer, and the family home continues as the principal residence of the transferee or transferees.”

Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2, opening of subdivision (a). California Legislature

“The exclusion ... shall not apply if the transferee or transferees do not occupy the family home as their principal residence within one year of the transfer.”

“If the transferee or transferees become ineligible ... the exclusion shall no longer apply and the property shall be subject to reassessment ... as of the date of the change in eligibility.”

Paraphrase of the operative conditions in section 63.2(a) and (d); the operative text is at the link. California Legislature

The value limit, and the amount in force

“... the taxable value of the family home ... shall be the sum of the following: (1) The taxable value ... determined as of the date of the transfer. (2) The amount, if any, by which the fair market value of the family home ... exceeds the sum of the taxable value determined under paragraph (1) and the applicable amount.”

Revenue and Taxation Code section 63.2(d)(2), as published. California Legislature

The statute sets the constitutional amount at one million dollars and indexes it every two years to a house price index. The Board of Equalization publishes the figure in force:

The relief equals the applicable amount in force, because the value at transfer exceeds the prior assessed value plus the amount. The first-year saving is that relief multiplied by the owner-implied rate, and it recurs every year the household stays.

The exclusion can be taken back. The exclusion is not free. It requires occupancy as a principal residence, and it is withdrawn if the household stops qualifying — including by moving out and renting the house, or by transferring it onward without a new claim. A household that takes the exclusion, lives in the house for two years and then lets it out does not keep the benefit; it gets a supplemental assessment and a bill for the difference. The claim itself is not a public document, but the exemption it depends on is.

The claim, and its three-year backstop

The statute allows a claim within three years of the transfer, or before the property is transferred to a third party, whichever comes first. A late claim may still be granted if the assessor is satisfied the household was eligible, but the safe course is to file in the first year. The claim is not a public document; the exemption that goes with it is a matter of record. California Legislature

What the exclusion is not

  • It is not a cap on the tax. The tax is still the local rate applied to the new taxable value.
  • It is not available for a second home, a rental, or a property the household does not occupy.
  • It is not automatic. The exemption claim is the trigger.
  • It is not the same as the older parent-child exclusion, which was unlimited. Proposition 19 replaced it for transfers on or after 16 February 2021.
  • It does not apply to Property B. That building is already owned and neither transfer nor occupancy changes its assessed value.
