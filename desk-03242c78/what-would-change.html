What would change the answer

A recommendation that cannot be overturned is not an analysis, it is a slogan. Each row below is one change to one input, with the model re-run and the ranking checked. Where the answer does not move, that is worth knowing too.

Short version
Two inputs can overturn the recommendation. Nothing else can.

The recommendation is selling A at the transfer date. It survives every tax change, every cost change, and a wide range of rents. It does not survive house prices growing faster than about 6.1% a year, and it does not survive a portfolio that cannot earn about 4.5% nominal. Both of those are plausible. Neither is the base case.

The two that matter are correlated. The two overturning inputs are not independent. House prices and equity returns tend to move together in the short run and diverge over decades, and a scenario where California house prices compound above 6.1% for twenty years while equities return less than 4.5% is conceivable but is not a central case. If the household believes it, the recommendation is to keep the house — and the model agrees, on those inputs.

What does not change the answer, and why that is useful

Almost every tax question on this desk turns out not to decide anything, because the same rule applies to both sides of the comparison. The step-up in basis applies to A whether the household keeps it or sells it. Proposition 19 applies to occupancy, and occupancy is a separate choice. Measure H applies to B regardless. Recognising which questions are decisive and which are merely interesting is most of the value of doing this at all.

The decisive questions, in order of how much they move the answer: what happens to house prices, what the portfolio earns, how long the household actually stays, and how much the household spends. The first two cannot be known. The third and fourth are choices. That is where attention belongs.
