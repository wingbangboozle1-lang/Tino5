Annual re-run, and the decision log

A twenty-year projection is not a decision made once. It is a set of inputs that will be wrong within a year. The discipline that makes this desk useful is re-running it every year and recording, next to each input, whether the recommendation moved.

The procedure

  1. Update every input marked owner or assumption. The workbook lists them all in one place. If a value has not changed, say so explicitly rather than silently reusing it.
  2. Re-run the model and rebuild the site. Both steps are one command each; the site is generated from the model, so a stale site is impossible if both are run.
  3. Compare the ranking to last year's. If the recommendation moved, record why, in one sentence, next to the input that moved.
  4. Check the watch list. Rates, the Proposition 19 amount, the ballot, the rent ordinance, and any federal proposal touching the step-up in basis.
  5. Record the decision and its date. Including a decision to do nothing. The log below is the record.

Watch list

What to check each year, and where to check it.
Item Why it matters Where to check Status
The Proposition 19 applicable amount Adjusts every two years and changes the value of occupancy. California State Board of Equalization
Treasury yields Set the return on parked proceeds and the alternative to a rental. U.S. Department of the Treasury
Consumer price index Drives the 2 percent property tax cap, rent growth and the inflation assumption. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics
Santa Clara County tax rate book The actual rate for Property A's tax rate area, which is still unverified. Santa Clara County Office of the Assessor
Measure H indexation Adjusts the parcel tax annually from fiscal 2028-29. San Francisco Department of Elections
Federal proposals on the step-up in basis Removal would change the arithmetic of every path on this desk. California Secretary of State
The rent ordinance Annual allowable increase and any change to owner move-in. not yet sourced
The re-run is cheap: two commands and about a minute. The reason it does not happen in most households is not cost, it is that no one owns it. Someone should.

This is a planning document, not advice. It is prepared for review by a licensed California attorney and a CPA before any step is taken. No legal, tax or investment advice is given or implied. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly and marked; every other figure is an assumption, an owner-supplied anchor, or arithmetic on those.

Nothing here identifies a person, a street address, an assessor's parcel number, or a ZIP code. Owner-supplied figures are approximate and unverified. Money is rounded to the nearest $10 except where a statute or official table states an exact amount.
