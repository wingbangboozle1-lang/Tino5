Income tax, basis, and the single most valuable fact in the file

A house inherited at death gets a new income-tax basis equal to its value on the date of death. Not an election, not a form, not a choice — it happens automatically. That one rule means a sale in the same tax year as the inheritance produces little or no taxable gain, and a sale twenty years later produces a very large one.

The consequence
Selling A within the same tax year as the inheritance is nearly tax-free. Waiting is a bet that costs income tax.

The basis resets to market value at death. A sale at that value produces no gain. Every year the household holds it, the basis stays fixed while the value grows, and the gap becomes taxable gain taxed at federal long-term capital gain rates plus net investment income tax plus California income tax — and California has no preferential capital gain rate at all. California Franchise Tax Board

The rates that apply to a gain

Every layer of tax on a long-term capital gain realised by a California resident at the top of the income range. All rates are statutory; only the underlying gain depends on assumption.
Layer Rate When it applies Source Status
Federal long-term capital gain 20% Taxable income above the 15% threshold; the 0% and 15% brackets are available at lower income and would reduce this layer. Internal Revenue Service
Net investment income tax 3.8% 3.8% of the lesser of net investment income or MAGI above $250,000 joint / $200,000 single. Internal Revenue Service
California income tax on the gain 12.3% Capital gain is taxed as ordinary income, with no preferential rate. California Franchise Tax Board
Behavioral Health Services Tax 1.0% 1% of taxable income above $1,000,000, reported on Form 540 line 62. California Legislature California Franchise Tax Board
Combined top marginal rate on a large gain 37.1% Used throughout this desk for a gain realised at the top of the range. sum of the four rows
Unrecaptured section 1250 gain 25% Depreciation taken on a rental, recaptured at up to 25% on sale, before the capital gain rate applies. Internal Revenue Service

Decision Value at sale Sale costs Section 121 used Depreciation recaptured Tax on sale Net proceeds Status

Sale costs are commission, documentary transfer tax and recording fees. The sale-at-transfer row shows no tax because the basis equals the sale price on the same date. The five-year row uses the section 121 exclusion because the household lived in the house; the letting row has no exclusion and pays recapture.

Recapture follows the rental. The letting row is the expensive one, and not for the reason people expect. The tax on the sale is $1,042,350, of which $1,300,310 is depreciation recapture — tax on a deduction the household took for years and now pays back at 25 percent. Letting the house does not avoid tax; it defers income tax at the cost of creating recapture. See depreciation and recapture.

What is not in this page

Three income-tax questions cannot be answered from the file and are listed as gaps rather than assumed away:

  • The trust's own income tax status. Whether the trust is a grantor trust, a complex trust, or something else changes who pays tax on income during administration and what the beneficiaries receive. The instrument was not read.
  • The household's marginal bracket at the time. The 20% federal rate assumes a high-income year. A household with little other income might pay 15% or, on part of the gain, nothing.
  • State residency at the time of sale. California taxes a nonresident on California-source gain, but the interaction with another state's rules is not modelled.
Each of these is a question for the CPA, with the specific document needed to answer it, on the CPA briefing sheet.
