Why the two properties are not comparable

The most common mistake in a case like this is to compare the two buildings as if they were the same kind of asset. They are not. One is a purchase the household already made and already pays tax on. The other is a transfer at death, which resets both the income-tax basis and, if nobody lives in it, the assessed value.

Four differences that change the arithmetic

The same two buildings, treated differently by four separate rules.
Question Property A (inherited) Property B (already owned) Consequence Status
What is the income-tax basis? Fair market value on the date of death, automatically, with no election. Internal Revenue Service Historic cost plus improvements less depreciation already taken. Not verified for this building. A can be sold soon after death with little or no gain. B cannot.
What is the assessed value? Reset to market on transfer unless the transferee moves in and claims the parent-child exclusion. California Legislature Carries its existing factored base year value; a change of residence inside the building is not a change of ownership. A costs far more to hold as a rental than B does, in tax terms, relative to its value.
Does living in it change the tax? Yes, decisively: occupancy is a condition of the Proposition 19 exclusion, worth up to about $1,044,586 of assessed value. California State Board of Equalization No. The household already owns it; occupancy changes no assessed value. Occupancy has a price on A and almost none on B.
What does selling it cost? Transfer tax at the county rate, recording fees, commission, and any capital gain not sheltered by the step-up. Transfer tax at the city rate too, plus a much larger taxable gain because the historic basis is low. Selling A is cheap in tax terms. Selling B is expensive. derived
The B sale path is under-taxed in this model. Selling B is modelled on this desk only as a comparison path, and it ranks last or next to last in every regime. The basis used for B's sale is its assessed value because the household's actual cost basis was not supplied. That makes B's sale look cheaper than it is: a building held for decades has a basis far below its assessed value, so the real taxable gain — and the real tax — are larger than modelled here.

Why the household's own housing must be counted

If living in A were scored as free housing and renting were scored as paying rent elsewhere, living would win by construction. The model avoids that. Every path carries the same housing consumption, valued at market rent: a household living in A is charged the rent it does not receive, and a household that sells everything is charged the rent it must pay. Shelter is reported as its own column so the reader can see how much of each outcome is housing rather than money.

Shelter is counted at market rent, not at imputed-rent tax rules. That is a deliberate simplification: the internal revenue code does not tax the rental value of a home the owner occupies, and it does not allow a deduction for rent paid on a personal residence. Counting both sides at market rent therefore cancels out and leaves a clean comparison. The tax consequences of each are modelled separately.
