Mixed paths: the three pure choices are not the only ones

A household rarely chooses to live in a house forever or sell it on day one. The realistic options combine decisions and sequence them. Six mixed paths were modelled against the three pure choices, all at the same dates and the same horizon.

What each mixed path adds

The six mixed paths, the decision each one adds, and what the model says it is worth against the closest pure choice.
Path What it adds Against Difference Verdict Status
P4 Live 5 years, then sell Occupancy to earn both the Proposition 19 exclusion and the section 121 exclusion, then liquidity. Selling at once -$2,729,320 Worse on the central case, better if growth is weak. derived
P4b Live 10 years, then sell The same, held longer. The section 121 cap binds, so the extra years add gain without adding exclusion. Selling at once -$5,614,810 Worse still on the central case; it is a long bet on the house. derived
P5 Let 10 years, then sell Rental income plus price growth, accepting recapture and no exclusion. Living 10 years, then selling -$1,663,800 Worse than living in it. The rent does not pay for the lost exclusions. derived
P6 Sell, park 24 months, then phase in Selling at once, with an explicit delay before market entry. Selling at once -$978,350 Costs a little in the central case; buys protection against a crash. derived
P6b Sell, park 36 months, then phase in The same with a longer parking period. Selling at once -$1,289,950 Costs a little more; the protection is longer. derived
P7 Sell both A and B Liquidity everywhere. The household rents. Selling A only -$7,623,750 Clearly worse. Selling B destroys sheltered cash flow and creates a taxable gain. derived
The paths with the most assumptions. The mixed paths are where the tax detail matters most, and where this desk is least certain. P4 and P4b depend on the household actually living in the house for the period the path assumes; the statute does not care about the plan, only the occupancy. P5 depends on a rent figure that is an unverified assumption. P6 and P6b depend on a Treasury yield that will not be the one observed on 13 November 2026 when the money actually arrives.

The sequencing question

Order matters independently of which paths are chosen. The model assumes a rational sequence; a real household faces a deadline. The order that minimises expensive mistakes:

  1. At the death: obtain the date-of-death appraisal, file the estate return if required, and do not let the house sit uninsured or unmaintained.
  2. Within one year: decide occupancy. This is the only decision with a hard statutory deadline and a large, irreversible tax consequence. California Legislature
  3. Before the money moves: write the investment policy. See policy for the proceeds.
  4. At the sale: sell, and do not park the proceeds in cash indefinitely while deciding. That is a decision too, and usually a bad one.
  5. After the sale: execute the policy, and re-run the model every year. See annual re-run.

This is a planning document, not advice. It is prepared for review by a licensed California attorney and a CPA before any step is taken. No legal, tax or investment advice is given or implied. Statutory amounts are quoted exactly and marked; every other figure is an assumption, an owner-supplied anchor, or arithmetic on those.

Nothing here identifies a person, a street address, an assessor's parcel number, or a ZIP code. Owner-supplied figures are approximate and unverified. Money is rounded to the nearest $10 except where a statute or official table states an exact amount.
