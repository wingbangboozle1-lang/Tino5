Decision at a glance

The inherited home (Property A) passes through a trust around 2040–2050. The heir already owns a home outright (Property B) and can live there without giving up rental income from Property B's two rented units (one unit is not rented). That distinction — one home inherited, one home already owned — drives everything.

Why selling is the baseline

  • On inheritance the home gets a step-up in basis to market value at death, so selling soon after transfer triggers little or no capital-gain tax.
  • Keeping the home means carrying it forever: property tax, insurance, water, electricity, maintenance, and — if rented — landlord work and vacancies.
  • Over long periods, a diversified stock portfolio has beaten metro home prices alone in most recorded 25-year periods (before rent, costs and tax).

What would change the answer (also on the landing page)

  • You move in within a year AND the Prop 19 cap still covers most of the market-to-assessment gap → living in can beat selling by locking in a low tax base.
  • Net rental yield is durably above ~4.5–5.5% after every cost and tax → renting can hold its own.
  • Stocks enter a 1929- or 2008-style crash right as you reinvest → the phased-investing path (Treasuries first, then stocks) is the protection; see Black swan.

Reinvestment risk beside the headline

Selling looks best on paper, but the return you actually earn depends on when the money goes back to work. If you reinvest the full proceeds into stocks at a market peak, a crash in the next 12–36 months can erase years of gains — that is reinvestment / sequence-of-returns risk. Parking the money in short Treasuries for 24–36 months before phasing into stocks reduces the crash entry risk; the cost is lower returns while parked. The what-if page lets you test both.
