Phased entry test: 1929 and 2008 crash risk

Whether to invest the proceeds in one go, or to park them in Treasury bills and buy in equal monthly tranches over 24 or 36 months. This page tests the entry risk. The mechanics are the same as the calculator on cbe/phased-investing.html, built in the owner-brief desk, which this desk links to rather than duplicates.

Each 'dollar' below is the proceeds of the sale, using the transfer-year figure of $6,139,360. The test runs over 10 years from entry, not to the 2076 horizon used on the paths page.

Break-even: how deep must the crash be?

A 24-month phase-in ends level with the lump sum after 10 years if the market is at or below 102.2% of its entry level at the 17-month trough. A 36-month phase-in breaks even at 100.4%. Above those levels, the lump sum wins; below them, the phase-in wins.

Break-even levels are computed by bisection on the model, not read from a published table.

How the model moves the market

  • Before the trough, the index moves from 100% to the trough level over 17 months. After the trough it grows at the net return of 6.40% a year.
  • Parked cash earns 2.56% a year after federal tax and the 3.8% surcharge, from the Treasury 3-month rate of 4.32% on 13 November 2026.
  • The 2008 fall (57%) and the 1929 fall (89%) are the official peak-to-trough figures from Federal Reserve History. The 1929 figure is for the Dow Jones Industrial Average, which has no dividends.

Reinvestment risk

Parked cash earns less than the market, and less still if bill rates fall. For the 36-month phase-in, a fall of 2 points in the cash rate after the first 12 months lowers the value at 2076 by $67,150. It is the price of waiting that matters, more than the risk of the rate itself.

Why the phase-in is not free

In the base case with no crash, the 36-month phase-in gives up $682,130 of present value against the lump sum. In the 2008-style case it gains $3,370,790. In the 1929-style case it gains $2,544,340. Whether that trade is worth making depends on the probability of a deep fall in the first few years, which this model does not estimate.
