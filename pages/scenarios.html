Options: live in it, rent it, or sell it

Each option is modelled to 2065 with identical assumptions, identical Property B cash flows, and all taxes included. Transfer is assumed in the middle of the stated window (2045), because Prop 19, the exclusion indexation and the federal exemption all move with the date.

The comparison

Reading the two totals

Net wealth counts only financial and property value. Plus housing consumed adds the market rent of the home each case occupies — this is what makes "live in it" comparable, because living in a $6.5M house is a real benefit that never appears in a bank account. Without that adjustment the live case looks worse than it is; with it, the live case still loses to selling, because it consumes a much more expensive house than the alternatives.

Why selling wins on the base case

  • Return spread. Property A is assumed to grow 4.0%/yr; the reinvested proceeds 6.0% before a 1.0% tax drag. Over 20 years that spread is worth millions.
  • No reassessment. A sale ends the property-tax question. Renting triggers a full reassessment to market value: — in 2045 against — if the exclusion is preserved.
  • No terminal capital-gains bill. A sale at death is tax-free on the stepped-up basis; a sale in 2065 from a 2045 basis carries ≈37.1% on two decades of gain plus depreciation recapture — — of proceeds at transfer versus a large tax later.
  • Concentration and liability. One house, one tenant, one wildfire-season insurance renewal, versus a diversified portfolio.

The one thing the numbers do not capture

Renting out a single-family house in Cupertino means becoming a landlord in one of the most tenant-protective states in the country, with habitability duties, rent-cap rules on some units, and liability that a brokerage account does not carry. That is a qualitative cost, and it is the strongest non-numeric argument for selling.
