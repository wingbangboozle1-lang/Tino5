The situation in one screen

Property A is a single-family home in Santa Clara County that the inheritor expects to receive through a trust in the 2040–2050 planning window. Property B is a three-unit building in San Francisco County that the same inheritor already owns and already rents out (two of three units). Property B is not part of the inheritance. Every rule, cost and tax consequence below keeps that distinction first: A is a future transfer whose terms are unknown today; B is an existing asset whose costs and income already run.

Reading the numbers. Owner-supplied figures are shown rounded to the nearest $10, as required by the brief, and are labelled owner-supplied — no official source can confirm a private household figure, so those rows carry a discovery action instead of a false citation. Official figures are printed as the agency publishes them with a link to the primary source. Projections and derived arithmetic are labelled derived and show their method. Nothing on this page names a person, address, parcel, ZIP code or account.

What the money does: the three paths for Property A

The distinction that drives everything: Property B is already owned, so its Proposition 13-protected assessment and its rental income continue unchanged under every option below. The decision is only about what happens to Property A at and after the transfer.

What the published models conclude today. The numeric desks rank sell ≥ rent > occupy on pure expected wealth in 15 of 17 stress runs, and the independent referee shows that the ranking is sensitive first to the assumed after-tax investment return (the two desks use 4.5% and 5.6%) and fails entirely if the alternative to the house is cash-like instruments. Read them side by side before deciding: numeric decision matrix (2040 / 2045 / 2050) · referee robustness checks · referee discrepancy log.

Recommendation (and the gates it is conditioned on)

Best current plan:

  1. Now → transfer (2026–2039): do nothing irreversible. Keep both properties as they are. Property B already combines a Proposition 13-protected low assessment with roughly $7,150 per month of rent; it is the base the plan builds on. Use the waiting period to close the information gaps — the question register exists for exactly this.
  2. At the transfer: the financial default is sell or rent, occupy only for living-value reasons. Every published desk and stress test so far puts selling ahead of renting ahead of occupying on expected wealth — but the ranking inverts if the alternative return is low, and occupying A is worth about $10,450 per year in property tax alone (base rate) versus renting it out, plus the avoided income tax on rent and the value of the housing itself. That trade is a lifestyle decision the numbers cannot make for you (Q-27 and Q-28).
  3. Before any 706-related or large-cash decision: inventory the estate. Combined owner estimates for the two properties total $4,900,000; against the 2026 exclusion of $15,000,000 that leaves about $10,100,000 of headroom before federal filing/tax can even apply — and the inherited cash is the missing variable (Q-06 and Q-07). If the gross estate exceeds the year-of-death exclusion (projected $15,480,000 for 2027), the unified rate schedule applies to the excess (Form 706, Table A); California adds nothing.
  4. Every recheck is scheduled: IRS restates the exclusion every November (next: late 2027); BOE restates the $1,044,586 cap every February of even years (next: February 2027); Prop 13's 2% cap is re-set annually; AB 1482 sunsets 1 January 2030. Monitor via the law-watch page and group 6.
Gate before any irreversible choice: the decision cannot be made "reasonably" until the red rows in the question register — trust terms, estate size and gift history, occupancy preference, insurance quotes, rentability facts and the return assumption — are answered. Pick your improvement path first on the suggestions page; nothing there is implemented without an explicit choice recorded on that page.
